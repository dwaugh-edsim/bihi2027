Would You?

Today we will examine several difficult moral dilemmas. There are no wrong answers, but you must be able to explain why you made your choice.
Before We Start

Three Ground Rules

1
Decide independently first
Take 10 seconds of silence before each vote. Decide your position before looking around the room.
2
Work within the scenario
Do not look for loopholes like yelling or untying knots. Choose between the specific options given.
3
Focus on the reasons
You are always free to change sides if a classmate offers a reason that persuades you.
Scenario Introduction

The Trolley Problem

First described by philosopher Philippa Foot in 1967. Today, this problem is directly studied by engineers programming decision systems in autonomous vehicles.
Scenario 1: Decide In Silence, Then Move

The Switch

A runaway trolley is hurtling toward five workers on the track. You are standing beside a lever. If you pull it, the trolley diverts onto a side track where one worker is tied. If you do nothing, the five workers die.
Variations: Move If Your Choice Changes (Clicker Next Advances)

What If?

Scenario 2: Decide In Silence, Then Move

The Footbridge

The same runaway trolley is heading toward the same five workers. There is no side track and no lever. You are standing on a footbridge above the rails next to a stranger wearing an enormous, heavy backpack. The backpack is heavy enough to stop the train. You are not. The only way to save the five workers is to push the stranger off the footbridge onto the tracks.
Scenario 3: Decide In Silence, Then Move

The Transplant

Five hospital patients each urgently require a different organ to survive. A healthy visitor arrives for a routine annual checkup and is a perfect match for all five patients. The doctor could sedate the visitor and use their organs to save all five lives. Nobody asks the visitor. Nobody would ever discover what occurred.

Partner Question (60 seconds): Why does pulling a lever feel morally different from pushing a person or operating on a visitor?

Three Frameworks

How Philosophers Categorize These Arguments

Outcomes
Consequentialism / Utilitarianism
Judge the action solely by the final result.
Saving 5 lives produces a better outcome than saving 1 life. If you pull the lever or push the stranger on this basis, you are evaluating total results.
Duties and Rules
Deontology (Kant)
Certain actions are wrong in themselves, regardless of results.
Using an innocent person as a physical tool or taking their life directly violates a fundamental moral duty. The outcome does not excuse the action.
Character
Virtue Ethics (Aristotle)
What kind of person does this choice make you?
Rather than calculating math or following rigid rules, ask what a person of integrity, compassion, and courage would do in this situation.
Which of these three frameworks best explains your own choices today?
