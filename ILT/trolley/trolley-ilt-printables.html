There are no right answers in this lesson. What is being assessed is whether you can explain why you chose. "I don't know yet" is a valid answer if you can say what's still bothering you. Write the reason, not just the choice — a choice on its own earns nothing.
Scenario 1 · The Switch
A runaway trolley is hurtling down a track. Five workers are tied to it. You are standing beside a lever. Pull it and the trolley diverts onto a side track, where one person is tied — the five on the main track live. Leave it and all five die.
A — Pull the lever
B — Do nothing
My reason — one sentence, not one word
Scenario 2 · The Footbridge
Same runaway trolley. The same five people tied to the track. But there is no lever and no side track. You are on a footbridge over the rails, and beside you is a large man. The only way to stop the trolley is to push him onto the track.
A — Push the man
B — Do nothing
My reason
Did your answer change from Scenario 1? Why?
Scenario 3 · The Surgeon
Five patients are in hospital. Each needs a different organ, and each will die without it. A healthy visitor arrives for a routine checkup — and happens to be a perfect match for all five. The surgeon gets a signed consent form and schedules the operation for tonight. One healthy person dies on the table. Five patients live.
A — Operate
B — Refuse
My reason
Stop and look at your own three answers. Same math three times. If your answer changed, you didn't change your mind about numbers — you changed your mind about something else. What was it? Hold that thought for the second page.

The Lifeboat — Profile Cards

Ten survivors · six spaces · print one set per group
Cut along the borders
Laminate if you plan to reuse
How to run it: Each group takes the full set of ten. You may not discard anyone before the argument starts. Your group must choose six to survive. Every person in the group argues for a different person — and your job is to argue for that specific person, not for a category of people. Whoever talks longest doesn't get a vote.

Alternative A · The Heinz Dilemma

Split the room: Yes / No / Can't Decide
A woman is dying. The medicine that will save her exists, in the pharmacy, right now. But the pharmacist will not sell it — the husband simply cannot pay. Every legal option has been tried and failed. There is a law against what he is considering doing. It would probably work. He might never be caught. Should he do it?
How to run it: Split the room into three physical groups — Yes, No, Can't Decide — and give each side five minutes to build the strongest possible case for the position it was assigned. Not for what they personally believe. Then one sentence from each side, no debate yet.

Alternative B · Small-stakes round

Same rules. Much smaller numbers.
Run these fast — thumbs up, thumbs sideways, thumbs down. No discussion yet.
Your best friend asks to copy your math test. Nobody will know.
You see a classmate steal a chocolate bar from the corner store.
The popular kids won't let you bring your unpopular friend along.
You find $40 in the school gym and nobody saw you pick it up.
The catch: these are easier — fewer lives, smaller numbers — and yet they are usually harder to decide. Have someone explain why. You are looking for the moment the trolley logic breaks down in real life.

Lesson Run Sheet

53 minutes · projector deck + these sheets
Room 8 · Bicentennial JHS
Arrows = stages · Space = timer · F = notes
Time Stage What you do
1Ground rules Read all four. Say the no-right-answers line out loud — it buys you the quiet half of the room. Stop anyone who asks "why not untie them?" One choice from the scenario as written.
4The Switch Hand vote, keep hands up, count into the deck's rail. Neutral face. Expect 80–90%.
4Pair talk Circulate. Eavesdrop. Find one student who can only count and one who can only talk about fairness — you'll call on both by name in Stage 7.
4The Footbridge Same vote. Do not rescue the room. Let the silence sit three seconds after the hands go down. The number on the rail is the whole lesson.
4The Surgeon Watch for the collapse toward "refuse." That collapse is the teaching moment.
5The pattern Push back hard on "everyone's different, it doesn't matter." Your answers weren't random — they changed when the how changed, not when the how many changed.
8Three lenses Name them. Then ask "which one is right?" and let the silence do the work. Gr 7: keep it to the three plain questions. Gr 9: use the philosopher names.
10Lifeboat groups Mix them yourself — no friend groups. Card set per group. Insist on the rule, not just the names.
6Small stakes Fast thumbs votes, then: why were the small ones harder?
4Exit ticket Silent, on their own, handed in on the way out. Read them tonight.
Failure mode to watch: the discussion collapsing into "there's no right answer so who cares." The counter is the pattern — the room already produced one, consistently, without noticing. Point at it.
If you run short: cut the Surgeon (Stage 3) and go straight from the Footbridge to the pattern. The Switch → Footbridge gap carries the entire lesson on its own; the Surgeon is reinforcement.

If you run long: swap the Lifeboat for the Heinz dilemma — the deck's stage-9 button flips it — and use the split-the-room version, which reports back faster.
