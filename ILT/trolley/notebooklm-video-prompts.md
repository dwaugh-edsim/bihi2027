# NotebookLM Video Prompts — ILT Ethics Lab

**How to use:** For each video, create a source (Add source → Copied text) with the **Source** text, then generate a Video Overview using the **Steering prompt**. Choose the *Brief* format if available.

**Universal rule for all of these:** the video sets up the dilemma and **stops at the question**. You run the vote and the discussion. Preview every video — if it hints at an answer or "explains the ethics," skip it and use the slide.

## 1. The Ring of Gyges (group discussion)

**Source**
> Around 2,400 years ago, the Greek philosopher Plato wrote down a story told by a character named Glaucon. A shepherd named Gyges is out with his flock when an earthquake opens a crack in the ground. Inside he finds a golden ring. Later, sitting with other shepherds, he twists the ring on his finger — and discovers that he has become completely invisible. Twist it back, and he reappears. Nobody can see him, nobody can catch him, and nobody will ever know what he does. Gyges uses the ring to take power for himself. Glaucon's challenge: anyone who found this ring would do the same. People are only "good" because they are afraid of getting caught. Take away the risk of getting caught, and goodness disappears.

**Steering prompt**
> Create a short, engaging video for 14-year-old students that tells the story of the Ring of Gyges from Plato. Tell it like a story: the shepherd, the earthquake, the ring, discovering invisibility. Briefly mention that Gyges used it to take power, but keep it non-violent and family-friendly. Then present Glaucon's challenge clearly: "People are only good because they're afraid of getting caught." Make it modern by asking viewers to imagine they found this ring today — at school, online, anywhere. Do NOT answer the question, do NOT say whether Glaucon is right, and do NOT mention any philosophers' conclusions or theories. End on two direct questions: "What would you do with the ring?" and "Are people only good because they might get caught?" Under 2 minutes. Plain, conversational language.

## 2. The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas (group discussion — alternative)

> [!NOTE]
> This is the softened version. Le Guin's original describes the child's condition in more detail; this keeps it at "alone and unhappy," which is appropriate for Grade 9.

**Source**
> In 1973, the writer Ursula K. Le Guin wrote a short story called "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas." Omelas is a beautiful city. Everyone there is happy — there is no war, no poverty, the music and festivals are wonderful, and people are kind to each other. But there is a secret. Somewhere under the city, in a small dark room, one child lives alone. The child is lonely and unhappy and never gets to go outside. Everyone in Omelas learns about the child when they are around your age. And everyone learns the deal: if anyone ever brings the child out, comforts them, or sets them free, all the happiness of Omelas disappears at once — for everyone. Most people, after they learn the secret, decide to stay. But a few people, quietly, walk out of the city and never come back.

**Steering prompt**
> Create a short, atmospheric video for 14-year-old students that sets up Ursula K. Le Guin's story "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas." Spend the first half making Omelas sound genuinely wonderful — a place viewers would want to live. Then reveal the secret about the one child, gently and without disturbing detail: the child is alone, lonely, and kept in a small dark room. Explain the deal clearly: free the child, and everyone's happiness ends. Mention that most people stay and a few walk away. Keep it calm and thoughtful, not scary. Do NOT say what the right choice is, do NOT interpret the story's meaning, and do NOT connect it to any specific real-world issue. End on the direct question: "Would you stay in Omelas — or walk away?" Under 2 minutes.

## 3. The Footbridge (optional — follow-up to the trolley video)

**Source**
> This is a second version of the Trolley Problem, created by philosopher Judith Jarvis Thomson. The same runaway trolley is heading toward the same five people. But this time there is no lever and no side track. You are standing on a footbridge above the track. Next to you is a stranger wearing an enormous, heavy backpack. The backpack is heavy enough to stop the trolley. You are not. There is no time to take the backpack off. The only way to stop the trolley is to push the stranger off the bridge onto the track. One person dies, five people live — exactly the same numbers as before.

**Steering prompt**
> Create a very short video for 14-year-old students presenting the Footbridge version of the Trolley Problem. Assume viewers have just seen the original lever version. Emphasize that the numbers are exactly the same — one dies, five live — and the only thing that has changed is HOW it happens. Keep it non-graphic. Do NOT describe what happens to the person in any detail, do NOT say how most people answer, and do NOT explain why people might answer differently. End on the question: "Would you push?" Under 90 seconds.

## 4. The Self-Driving Car (optional — extension / next class)

**Source**
> Self-driving cars are already on the road in some cities. Engineers have to program them to make decisions in emergencies — before the emergency ever happens. Imagine a self-driving car whose brakes fail. If it keeps going straight, it will hit a group of five pedestrians crossing the road. If it swerves, it will crash into a wall, and the one passenger inside the car will be hurt. The car cannot decide in the moment the way a human would. Someone has to decide in advance, in a computer lab, and write it into the code. Researchers at MIT created a website called the Moral Machine where millions of people around the world answered questions like this, and people in different countries often answered differently.

**Steering prompt**
> Create a short, energetic video for 14-year-old students connecting the Trolley Problem to self-driving cars. Explain that engineers must program the car's choices in advance. Present the brakes-fail scenario clearly and non-graphically (use words like "hit" and "hurt", nothing more). Mention that MIT's Moral Machine asked millions of people and that answers varied around the world, but do NOT say which answers were most common. Raise the extra twist: "Would you buy a car that was programmed to sacrifice YOU to save five strangers?" Do NOT give answers or recommend a choice. End on: "If you were the engineer, what would you program the car to do?" Under 2 minutes.
