# Integrated Learning Time (ILT) Classroom Activity Ideas for Grade 7–9

## Overview
Integrated Learning Time (ILT) in HRCE is defined as two 60‑minute blocks or one 120‑minute block per 10‑day cycle, embedded within provincial Time to Learn and intended for student‑centred, inquiry‑based, cross‑curricular learning. The ILT block emphasizes essential competencies such as critical thinking, collaboration, communication, reasoning, and synthesis, with teachers acting as facilitators and students actively engaged in questioning, investigating, and communicating their learning.[^1][^2]

ILT samples collected across HRCE schools show that effective ILT often runs as ongoing modules (4–8 weeks) where students pursue an essential question through project‑based or inquiry‑based activities that integrate multiple subject outcomes and culminate in a shareable product or performance.[^3]

## Design Principles for ILT Activities

### Alignment with HRCE ILT expectations

HRCE guidance frames ILT as student‑centred learning tied to curriculum outcomes, with entry points tailored to individual strengths and needs and explicit attention to skills and competencies within renewed curricula. Teachers are expected to collect assessment evidence through conversations, observations, and products, aligned with curriculum outcomes across subject areas.[^1]

ILT can occur in a variety of spaces (classrooms, cafeteria, hallways, outside, labs or gym) and should feel productive, with multiple concurrent activities and clear norms for movement, collaboration, and use of materials. Scheduling options include whole‑school ILT, grade‑level ILT, or specific teacher‑led ILT blocks, with collaboration time built into prep for co‑planning modules.[^1]

### Inquiry‑based and project‑based structure

Inquiry‑based learning in middle years places a driving or essential question at the centre of a multi‑session module and has students answer it through guided investigation, hands‑on work, and structured reflection. Effective guided inquiry for middle school follows phases such as Connect/Wonder, Investigate, Construct/Express, and Reflect, with the teacher providing the question and scaffolds while students make choices about sources, methods, and products.[^4][^5][^6][^7]

Project‑based learning modules for grades 7–9 typically unfold over several weeks, with repeated cycles of Engage, Explore, Explain, Elaborate, and Evaluate that link to real‑world contexts and culminate in authentic products (presentations, exhibits, websites, prototypes, community proposals).[^7][^8]

### Class size and logistics (28‑student classroom)

HRCE samples show that ILT projects are commonly run with full junior‑high classes using small groups (3–5 students), rotating teacher facilitation, and flexible seating or alternative spaces to manage materials and collaboration. Teachers emphasize clear criteria, timelines, and routines (movement breaks, station work, check‑ins) to keep large classes productive while students work on different tasks simultaneously.[^9][^3][^1]

## HRCE ILT Activity Patterns from Sample Schools

### Career and lifestyle exploration (Leslie Thomas JH)

One school implemented a career and lifestyle exploration unit where students used web‑based tools to explore personality traits, lifestyle goals, and future work opportunities, culminating in Google Sites that compiled their findings. Essential questions such as “What career is best for me?”, “How do I get there?”, and “What lifestyle will this career allow me to afford?” guided the inquiry, anchored in English Language Arts outcomes about creating oral, written, and visual communication for different audiences.[^3]

Assessment relied on teacher conferencing, small‑group feedback, and checklists or rubrics for the final websites, with sharing in small groups to provide an authentic audience. The activity promoted student choice of careers and lifestyles, keeping them interested and personally invested.[^3]

### Multi‑module skills and coping strategies (Oyster Pond Academy)

Another school ran year‑long ILT modules on mental and physical coping strategies, a “Space Race” egg‑drop design challenge, growth mindset, and a school design challenge, each lasting six to eight weeks. These modules targeted outcomes in Health, Technology Education, and ELA, and were explicitly designed to develop core curriculum skills such as constructing solutions to design challenges, analyzing health behaviours, and planning oral and written communication.[^3]

Each module used essential questions (e.g., “What were some problems you encountered in your design challenge?”, “What is the difference between a growth mindset and a fixed mindset?”) and culminated in events like student‑led expos to share designs and findings. Teachers noted the importance of explicit skill instruction (e.g., understanding continuum skills, growth mindset, collaboration) and balanced physical, social, and cognitive activity in each module.[^3]

### Future cities and sustainability (Brookside JH)

A Future Cities project had students explore how culture, climate, population and technology shape sustainable living and housing, integrating Technology and Science outcomes on design and climate change. Essential questions such as “What is a city?”, “What is sustainability?”, and “Why is location important for city planning?” structured mini‑lessons and guided research.[^3]

Students worked individually or in small groups to create culminating future‑city models or representations using any materials or technology available, then showcased their work in classroom expos visited by other classes. Assessment included exit slips, reflection sheets, and observation checklists, and the school identified the benefit of distributing building and creation across the module instead of concentrating it at the end for better assessment opportunities.[^3]

### Community improvement and social action (Leslie Thomas JH, Cunard JH)

Community‑focused ILT modules asked students to identify unmet needs in the local community and design products or services to address them, integrating English and Social Studies outcomes on communication and citizenship. Essential questions such as “How can we create positive change in our community and convince others to get on board?” drove research into beneficiaries, costs, funding, locations, and persuasive strategies.[^3]

Students typically worked in small groups or individually to design websites, presentations or prototypes that were shared with classmates or at events like multicultural fairs, with assessment through forms, rubrics, and conferences. Teachers noted that student choice of topics and partners, plus movement breaks and planned physical activities, helped maintain engagement across large classes.[^3]

### Engineering and design challenges (multiple schools)

Several ILT samples focused on engineering and design challenges, such as building bridges linked to a science unit on engineering structures, fluid‑power devices to lift bricks, or upcycled‑materials towers and bridges. These experiences integrated Technology and Science outcomes on constructing solutions to design challenges, understanding forces, and distinguishing hydraulics from pneumatics.[^3]

Assessment was largely formative, using descriptive feedback, open‑ended questioning (“What does a successful structure look like?”), and group testing or competitions to evaluate designs. Teachers emphasized teamwork, iteration, and problem‑solving as key skills, and recommended adjustments like longer building stages, smaller class sizes, or more explicit criteria to improve future runs.[^3]

### Passion projects and personal inquiries (Five Bridges JH, Sackville Heights JH)

Passion project ILT modules combined teacher‑led “passion sessions” (coding, martial arts, sustainable clothing, structure building, art) with student‑designed projects in which learners chose topics such as sports, art styles, scientific concepts, hobbies, or social issues like Ukraine. Outcomes included ELA communication and Healthy Living analysis of life skills, with essential questions like “What are my interests or hobbies?” and “How could I share this information in an interesting way?” guiding the work.[^3]

Students presented projects in varied formats—Google Slides, demonstrations, art pieces, videos, and student‑guided activities—and received descriptive feedback at each step rather than grades, promoting risk‑taking and ownership. Teachers highlighted the need for more explicit assessment structure and grouping students with facilitators knowledgeable about their topics in future iterations.[^3]

## Activity Design Framework for a 28‑Student ILT Classroom

### Core structural choices

For a 28‑student class, ILT activities are more manageable when organized around:

- Small groups of 3–4 students with specific roles (researcher, designer, communicator, organizer) to distribute responsibility and allow peer support.[^7][^3]
- Clear essential or driving questions that connect to students’ lives and curriculum outcomes, posted visibly and revisited each week.[^5][^1]
- A multi‑week sequence (4–8 ILT sessions) using inquiry phases such as Ask/Connect, Investigate/Explore, Create/Communicate, and Reflect/Evaluate.[^4][^7]
- A culminating product or event (expo, digital portfolio, community presentation, competition, or simulation) that provides an authentic audience beyond the teacher.[^8][^3]

### Sample weekly sequence template

A practical ILT module template for middle school could follow this pattern:

1. **Week 1 – Engage and define the question.** Use a discrepant event, case study, or local issue to hook interest; co‑construct an essential question; form groups; begin brainstorming.[^5][^7]
2. **Week 2 – Investigate sources and models.** Teach or model research skills, source evaluation, and note‑taking; have groups collect examples, data, or case studies linked to the essential question.[^6][^4]
3. **Week 3 – Ideate and plan.** Groups generate possible solutions, designs, or arguments; sketch plans; identify materials or information needed; agree on roles and timelines.[^7]
4. **Week 4 – Build, draft, and test.** Students construct prototypes, draft written pieces, or create digital artifacts; run small tests or peer reviews; revise based on feedback.[^9][^3]
5. **Week 5 – Finalize and prepare to share.** Groups refine products, plan presentations, and rehearse; teacher supports with mini‑lessons on communication skills.[^7][^3]
6. **Week 6 – Showcase and reflect.** Products are shared with peers or external audiences; students reflect on both their learning and the inquiry process, using prompts about challenges, successes, and next steps.[^4][^3]

## Concrete ILT Activity Ideas for Grade 7–9

### 1. Local Water Quality Inquiry (Science, Social Studies, ELA)

**Driving question:** “Is the water in our community safe for recreation and drinking?”[^5][^7]

**Curricular links:** Science outcomes on water systems, pollutants and health; Social Studies outcomes on community infrastructure and environmental responsibility; ELA outcomes on informational writing and oral presentation.[^5][^7]

**Activity outline (6+ ILT sessions):**

- Session 1: Introduce the local context (e.g., nearby stream, lake, or municipal water supply) and discuss students’ experiences; co‑create the essential question and sub‑questions.[^5]
- Session 2: Teach basic concepts about water pollutants, testing parameters (pH, dissolved oxygen, nitrates, phosphates), and how to read public water‑quality data; groups plan investigations.[^7][^5]
- Session 3: Conduct field sampling (if feasible) or analyze secondary data sets; students record measurements, observations and initial interpretations.[^5]
- Session 4: Groups compare their findings to published standards (e.g., Health Canada or EPA guidelines), identify patterns, and begin drafting claims with evidence.[^7]
- Session 5: Students create data reports, infographics or short persuasive briefs addressed to a simulated audience (e.g., town council, recreation board), recommending actions or precautions.[^5][^7]
- Session 6: Hold a “Water Quality Forum” where groups present findings; peers ask questions; students reflect on scientific reasoning, civic responsibility, and how inquiry changed their views.[^7][^5]

**Classroom logistics:** In a 28‑student class, form 7 groups of 4; assign roles such as data specialist, mapper, writer, and presenter; rotate teacher attention among groups and use simple checklists for formative assessment.[^5][^7]

### 2. Future‑Ready City Design (Technology, Science, Social Studies, Art)

**Driving question:** “How can we design a future city that is sustainable and livable for everyone?”[^10][^3]

**Curricular links:** Technology Education outcomes on constructing solutions to design challenges; Science outcomes on climate change and energy; Social Studies outcomes on citizenship and urban planning; Visual Arts outcomes on design and representation.[^10][^3]

**Activity outline (5–8 ILT sessions):**

- Session 1: Explore examples of innovative cities (e.g., eco‑cities, smart cities) through images or short videos; brainstorm what makes a city sustainable and livable; co‑create criteria.[^10]
- Session 2: Teach mini‑lessons on climate impacts, renewable energy, transportation, and housing equity; groups choose a future city context (real or imagined) and focus (coastal, northern, space colony, etc.).[^10][^3]
- Session 3: Groups sketch city layouts and make design decisions about transportation networks, energy sources, green spaces, and social services; link choices back to scientific and social concepts.[^10][^3]
- Session 4–5: Students build models or digital representations using cardboard, recyclables, or simple digital tools; embed labels or short explanations for features tied to sustainability criteria.[^9][^3]
- Session 6: Host a “Future Cities Expo” where classes visit each other’s displays and complete brief exit slips commenting on innovative ideas and potential improvements.[^3]
- Session 7: Whole‑class debrief about trade‑offs, unintended consequences, and how city design influences citizens’ quality of life.[^11][^3]

**Classroom logistics:** With 28 students, use 6–8 project teams, each responsible for one city; manage materials via station bins; give each team a large baseboard or digital canvas; build in time for setup and cleanup at the start and end of each ILT period.[^9][^3]

### 3. Community Change Lab (Citizenship, ELA, Math)

**Driving question:** “How can we create positive change in our community and convince others to support it?”[^3]

**Curricular links:** Social Studies outcomes on citizenship and community organizations; ELA outcomes on persuasive and multimodal texts; Math outcomes on basic budgeting and data representation.[^7][^3]

**Activity outline (5–7 ILT sessions):**

- Session 1: Students brainstorm local issues (e.g., food insecurity, transit, youth spaces, environmental concerns) and vote on several themes; small groups select a specific problem.[^3]
- Session 2: Teach how to research local organizations, programs and policies; groups gather information and identify gaps or unmet needs.[^7][^3]
- Session 3: Groups design a solution (program, event, service or physical improvement), sketching who benefits, partners, costs and logistics; introduce simple budgeting.[^3]
- Session 4: Students create persuasive products (websites, posters, slide decks, or short videos) targeted at a relevant audience (school leadership, local councillor, community association).[^9][^3]
- Session 5: Run a “Community Pitch Day” where groups present and receive peer feedback on feasibility, impact, and clarity.[^3]
- Session 6: Reflect on citizenship competencies, communication strategies, and next steps (e.g., sending a summary letter or implementing a small‑scale version).[^7][^3]

**Classroom logistics:** In a 28‑student group, form 7 teams of 4; use visible timelines and criteria; schedule one movement‑based activity or energizer mid‑session to maintain focus; integrate digital tools like Google Sites or Slides for low‑prep publishing.[^9][^3]

### 4. Personal Passion and Skill Project (ELA, Healthy Living, Arts)

**Driving question:** “What am I curious to learn more about, and how can I share it in an engaging way?”[^6][^3]

**Curricular links:** ELA outcomes on creating oral, written, and visual communication for varied audiences; Healthy Living outcomes on life skills influencing health; Arts or Technology outcomes depending on chosen topics.[^6][^3]

**Activity outline (6+ ILT sessions):**

- Session 1: Use interest inventories and quick “lightning talks” by teachers or peers to surface a wide range of possible topics; discuss the difference between narrow, factual questions and broader, open‑ended inquiries.[^12][^3]
- Session 2: Students draft essential questions (e.g., “How can I design a hockey stick that suits my playing style?”, “How are people in Ukraine experiencing the war?”) and refine them using questioning frameworks such as Bloom or Costa levels.[^13][^3]
- Session 3: Teach research strategies (search modifiers, evaluating sources, giving credit) and have students collect information or practice skills related to their question.[^6][^3]
- Session 4–5: Students create products aligned with their interest—demonstrations, art pieces, instructional videos, mini‑exhibitions, or performances—incorporating evidence or skill demonstrations.[^3]
- Session 6: Present projects to classmates in whole‑class or small‑group formats based on comfort level and receive peer questions and feedback.[^3]
- Session 7: Reflect on personal growth, resilience, and how inquiry can apply to other subjects or life decisions.[^6][^3]

**Classroom logistics:** In a 28‑student class, allow individual or pair‑based projects; schedule structured check‑ins (e.g., “proposal”, “mid‑point progress”, “draft product”) with simple conferencing templates for formative feedback; manage diverse materials by having students submit resource requests in advance.[^6][^3]

### 5. Engineering Structures and Fluid Power Challenge (Science, Technology, Math)

**Driving question:** “How can we design a structure or device that meets specific performance criteria?”[^10][^3]

**Curricular links:** Science outcomes on forces, structures, and hydraulics; Technology Education outcomes on designing and constructing devices; Math outcomes on measurement and data analysis.[^10][^3]

**Activity outline (4–6 ILT sessions):**

- Session 1: Introduce a design challenge (e.g., build a bridge of limited materials to span a set distance; design a hydraulic device that lifts a set weight; construct a tower from upcycled materials meeting height and stability criteria). Present success criteria and constraints.[^9][^3]
- Session 2: Teach mini‑lessons on forces, structural shapes (triangles, trusses), and fluid power principles; students examine examples and discuss why certain designs work.[^10][^3]
- Session 3: Groups plan and sketch designs, list materials and procedures, and predict performance; teacher circulates asking open‑ended questions about design trade‑offs.[^9][^3]
- Session 4–5: Build and test prototypes; collect data (e.g., maximum load, height, number of attempts); encourage iteration and modification based on test results.[^10][^3]
- Session 6: Hold a structured competition or showcase, then debrief on teamwork, problem‑solving, and how scientific concepts informed design decisions.[^3]

**Classroom logistics:** With 28 students, form 7 groups of 4; assign roles (materials manager, builder, recorder, tester); set up stations for building and testing; establish safety norms and cleanup routines; limit material types to keep costs manageable.[^9][^3]

### 6. Debate and Argumentation Lab (Social Studies, ELA)

**Driving question:** “How can we use evidence and reasoning to argue about a controversial issue fairly and effectively?”[^12][^3]

**Curricular links:** Social Studies outcomes on historical and contemporary issues; ELA outcomes on argument, rhetoric, and critical reading.[^11][^12]

**Activity outline (5–6 ILT sessions):**

- Session 1: Introduce a controversial historical or civic issue (e.g., a local development decision, a historical conflict) and model inquiry questions that go beyond factual recall (e.g., “Was this policy necessary?”).[^12]
- Session 2: Teach how to examine multiple sources (primary and secondary) and identify bias; students read contrasting accounts and generate deep questions.[^11][^12]
- Session 3: Groups develop claims for different positions, supported by evidence; co‑construct criteria for effective argument (clarity, evidence quality, respect).[^12][^11]
- Session 4: Run structured debates or Socratic seminars with speaking and listening norms; peers track use of evidence and reasoning.[^6][^9]
- Session 5: Students individually write argumentative pieces synthesizing multiple perspectives and reflecting on how inquiry shaped their views.[^11][^12]
- Session 6: Reflect on discussion norms, emotional complexity of controversial topics, and how to move from debate to action.[^8][^12]

**Classroom logistics:** In a 28‑student class, use inner‑circle/outer‑circle seminar formats or multiple simultaneous small‑group debates; assign observer roles to monitor evidence use; employ simple rubrics that focus on participation and reasoning rather than winning.[^8][^6]

## Assessment and Feedback Strategies

### Evidence collection aligned with outcomes

HRCE guidance recommends that assessment evidence in ILT be drawn from conversations, observations, and products, and be explicitly aligned to targeted curriculum outcomes and skills. Many sample schools used tools such as rubrics, checklists, Google Forms, exit slips, reflection sheets, and conferencing notes to capture learning during ILT modules.[^1][^3]

To keep assessment manageable in a 28‑student class, teachers can focus on a limited set of competencies per module (e.g., communication and critical thinking) and design rubrics or success criteria specific to the essential question and product type.[^6][^3]

### Feedback, reflection, and iteration

Descriptive feedback and open‑ended questioning (e.g., “What does a successful structure look like?”) were common in engineering and teamwork‑focused ILT samples and helped students refine their thinking rather than chase marks. Reflection prompts at the end of modules (e.g., “What would you do differently next time?”, “How did your understanding of this skill change?”) supported metacognitive awareness and planning for future improvements.[^4][^3]

Inquiry‑based learning guides emphasize structured reflection on both content and process as a final phase, encouraging students to assess how well their product answers the driving question and what they learned about learning itself.[^4][^7]

### Managing assessment load in large classes

In a 28‑student classroom, teachers can manage assessment by:

- Using group‑level rubrics for products while capturing individual evidence through brief conferences and reflection journals.[^6][^3]
- Rotating which groups receive detailed feedback each session and maintaining lightweight tracking sheets for participation and key skills.[^3]
- Prioritizing performance‑based assessments (presentations, demonstrations, prototypes) that allow simultaneous observation of multiple competencies.[^11][^3]

## Implementation Considerations for Grade 7–9 ILT

### Scheduling and collaboration

HRCE suggests that ILT scheduling can vary (full‑school, grade‑level, or specific teachers) and that administrators should develop internal plans that include collaboration time among ILT teachers, often using prep time blocks for co‑planning and reflection. Sample schools reported alternating ILT blocks to support shared teaching responsibilities and adjusting modules over the year based on student needs and teacher learning.[^1][^3]

For grade 7–9 teams, collaborative planning might involve agreeing on common skills to emphasize (e.g., communication, technological fluency, creativity/innovation) and distributing those skills across modules so students encounter them in different contexts.[^3]

### Equity, accessibility, and student‑centred qualities

HRCE ILT guidance and school samples stress Universal Design for Learning: providing varied entry points, multiple means of representation and expression, and opportunities to be physically, socially, and cognitively active. Activities such as passion projects, community improvement labs, and movement breaks during ILT time were used to support diverse learners and address skill gaps exacerbated by disruptions like COVID‑19.[^1][^3]

Inquiry‑based learning research for middle school highlights the importance of scaffolds (question stems, source templates, research notebooks) so that all students—regardless of prior achievement—can participate meaningfully in questioning and investigation.[^14][^6]

### Continuous improvement of ILT modules

Several HRCE schools explicitly reflected on how they would revise their ILT experiences, citing needs for more project‑based continuity, better balance of skills across modules, more explicit assessment structures, and increased student autonomy over time. Teachers involved in year‑long inquiry work noted that as their own understanding deepened, they moved gradually toward more guided and free inquiry, allowing students greater choice of questions and methods.[^6][^3]

Ongoing revision can be supported by simple post‑module reviews where ILT teachers share what worked, what did not, and how student feedback or outcomes suggest changes to essential questions, timelines, materials, or assessment approaches in future years.[^1][^3]
