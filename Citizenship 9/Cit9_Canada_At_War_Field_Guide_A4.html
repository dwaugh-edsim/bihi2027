THE FOUR MAJOR WARS COMMON KNOWLEDGE BASE
1. World War I 1914–1918 ~620,000 SERVED • ~66,000 KILLED
What the war was actually about: Rival European empires spent decades building giant armies and secret alliances like a line of dominoes. When a Serbian nationalist assassinated the Austrian Archduke in 1914, the dominoes fell and dragged dozens of countries into four years of slaughter in the mud. Canada was a British colony with no separate foreign policy, so when Britain declared war, all Canadians were automatically at war too.
Where it was fought: The Western Front across trenches in France and Belgium (Vimy Ridge, Ypres, the Somme).
Who we fought: The German Empire and the Austro-Hungarian Empire.
How it ended: Allied forces broke the German lines in 1918, ending with an armistice on November 11.
Nova Scotia connection: Halifax was the main port where troops sailed for Europe. On Dec 6, 1917, the war came home when the munitions ship Mont-Blanc blew up, killing nearly 2,000 people.
2. World War II 1939–1945 ~1,100,000 SERVED • ~45,000 KILLED
What the war was actually about: Adolf Hitler and Nazi Germany, joined by fascist Italy and imperial Japan, tried to conquer Europe and Asia, crushing democracies and murdering six million Jewish people in the Holocaust. This time, Canada was an independent country: Parliament debated for a week and voted to enter the war on our own. Canada converted its whole economy to fight, building ships, tanks, and bombers.
Where it was fought: Atlantic Ocean, Europe (Italy, Normandy on D-Day, the Netherlands), and the Pacific (Hong Kong).
Who we fought: The Axis Powers (Nazi Germany, Fascist Italy, Imperial Japan).
How it ended: Germany surrendered in May 1945 after Berlin was captured; Japan surrendered in August 1945 after two atomic bombs.
Nova Scotia connection: Halifax was the Allied Convoy Capital. Merchant ships assembled in Bedford Basin to cross the Atlantic under constant German U-boat attack.
3. The Korean War 1950–1953 ~26,000 SERVED • 516 KILLED
What the war was actually about: During the Cold War, the world was split between the Communist bloc (Soviet Union and China) and Western democracies (US, Canada, and allies). Korea was divided into a Communist North and a pro-Western South. In June 1950, the North launched a surprise invasion that almost pushed the South into the sea. The United Nations sent troops to stop the takeover. After three brutal years in freezing mountain trenches, the war ended in a stalemate.
Where it was fought: The Korean Peninsula, especially along the rugged 38th Parallel and the Battle of Kapyong.
Who we fought: North Korean communist forces and the Chinese People's Volunteer Army.
How it ended: An armistice (ceasefire) was signed on July 27, 1953. No peace treaty was ever signed; a heavily armed border (DMZ) still splits Korea today.
Legacy: Known as "The Forgotten War" because it sat in the shadow of WWII, yet it saw intense, deadly combat in extreme sub-zero weather.
4. The War in Afghanistan 2001–2014 ~40,000 SERVED • 158 SOLDIERS KILLED
What the war was actually about: On September 11, 2001, Al-Qaeda terrorists hijacked four airliners and attacked New York and Washington, killing nearly 3,000 civilians, including 24 Canadians. Al-Qaeda was based in Afghanistan under the protection of the Taliban government. Canada joined NATO allies under Article 5 to remove the Taliban from power and prevent future attacks. Canadian troops spent over a decade fighting insurgents in the dangerous dust of Kandahar.
Where it was fought: Kandahar Province, Afghanistan, in baking heat, mountain valleys, and grape fields.
Who we fought: Taliban insurgents, Al-Qaeda fighters, and roadside bomb (IED) networks.
How it ended: Canadian combat operations ended in 2011, and training missions wrapped up in 2014. Thousands of veterans returned with physical injuries and severe PTSD.
Nova Scotia connection: Private Nathan Smith of Tatamagouche was killed at Tarnak Farm in 2002: one of Canada's first combat deaths since Korea.
WHO SERVED IN THE SHADOWS? CITIZENSHIP AND RECOGNITION

No. 2 Construction Battalion (Nova Scotia's Black Battalion)

When WWI began in 1914, Black Canadians rushed to enlist but were turned away at recruiting offices by white officers. Black leaders fought back, demanding the right to serve. In 1916, Canada created the No. 2 Construction Battalion, headquartered in Pictou and Halifax. Over 600 Black men served in France, harvesting timber and building supply lines under artillery fire. In 2022, the Prime Minister delivered an official national apology in Truro for the anti-Black racism they faced.

Indigenous Veterans: Service Without Equal Rights

Over 7,000 First Nations, Inuit, and Métis soldiers volunteered in WWI and WWII, serving as decorated snipers, scouts, and code talkers (like Francis Pegahmagabow and Tommy Prince). Yet when they returned home, they faced shocking inequality: they were still denied the right to vote in federal elections, could not enter Legion halls, and were denied equal access to veteran land grants and benefits under the federal Indian Act.

CANADA AS A PEACEKEEPER FOUR MISSIONS EVERY CITIZEN SHOULD KNOW

1. Suez Crisis (1956): The Birth of Peacekeeping

The Crisis: Britain, France, and Israel launched a secret military invasion of Egypt to take back the Suez Canal. The Soviet Union threatened nuclear strikes, and the world teetered on the brink of World War III.

The Solution: Canadian diplomat Lester B. Pearson proposed sending an impartial international force in blue helmets to stand between the armies so the invaders could pull out without a fight. It worked, Pearson won the Nobel Peace Prize, and Canada became known as the world's chief peacekeeper.

2. Cyprus (1964–Present): The Long Stand-Off

The Crisis: Violence exploded on the Mediterranean island of Cyprus between Greek Cypriots and Turkish Cypriots. Canadian peacekeepers were sent to patrol the buffer zone (the "Green Line") separating the communities.

The Reality: In 1974, Turkey invaded the island. Canadian peacekeepers were caught in the crossfire, defending Nicosia Airport under heavy artillery. Over 30 years, 28 Canadian soldiers were killed holding the ceasefire line in Cyprus.

3. The Balkans / Bosnia (1992–1995): Under Fire

The Crisis: When Yugoslavia broke apart, vicious ethnic civil war broke out. Canadian peacekeepers were sent to protect civilians and escort food convoys through sniper fire and mortar barrages.

The Medak Pocket (1993): Croatian troops attacked Serbian villages under Canadian protection. Soldiers of the 2nd Battalion PPCLI fought a fierce 14-hour pitched battle to stop the slaughter. It was Canada's largest battle between Korea and Afghanistan, leaving soldiers with severe trauma.

4. Rwanda (1994): The Tragic Limit of Peacekeeping

The Crisis: Extremists in Rwanda launched a coordinated genocide against the Tutsi minority, murdering roughly 800,000 innocent people in just 100 days.

The Moral Stand: Canadian General Roméo Dallaire commanded the tiny UN mission. He warned world leaders and begged for reinforcements to stop the killings. Instead, the UN ordered him to withdraw. Dallaire stayed behind with a few hundred soldiers and saved tens of thousands of lives, but the world's refusal to act exposed the limits of peacekeeping.

HOME FRONT NOVA SCOTIA WHEN THE WAR REACHED OUR DOORSTEP

Halifax Explosion (December 6, 1917)

Halifax was the busiest war port in North America, packed with troopships and munitions. The French freighter SS Mont-Blanc, carrying 2,900 tonnes of high explosives, collided with the Norwegian ship SS Imo in the Narrows. The explosion wiped out the North End, flattened houses for miles, killed almost 2,000 people, and injured 9,000. It remains the deadliest disaster on Canadian soil.

Battle of the Atlantic: Life on the Front Line (1939–1945)

Halifax and Sydney were the nerve centres for transatlantic convoys delivering food, fuel, and weapons to Britain. German U-boats prowled right off the Nova Scotia coast, sinking merchant vessels in the Gulf of St. Lawrence and the Cabot Strait (such as the ferry SS Caribou, killing 137 people). Harbour anti-submarine nets, blackout curtains on home windows, and coastal searchlights were everyday life.

The VE Day Riots (May 7–8, 1945)

Halifax was exhausted by six years of wartime overcrowding, food rationing, and tight alcohol laws. When victory in Europe was announced on May 7, over 10,000 sailors, soldiers, and civilians flooded downtown. Frustration boiled over into chaos: liquor stores were looted, streetcars overturned, and shop windows smashed across Barrington and Gottingen streets before calm was restored.

Bedford Magazine Explosion (July 18–19, 1945)

Just ten weeks after VE Day, a fire broke out on an ammunition jetty at Magazine Hill on the Bedford Basin. A massive barge loaded with depth charges blew up, setting off chain-reaction explosions among 2,000 tonnes of bombs. The sky turned black as artillery shells rained over the harbour. Halifax and Dartmouth were evacuated; volunteer firefighters and naval ratings worked through the blasts to keep the main bunkers from blowing.

YOUR REMEMBRANCE DAY TASK HOW TO USE THIS FIELD GUIDE
Connecting to Your 300-Word Report (Google Classroom):
Whether you choose Option A (interviewing your family about a relative who lived through wartime, military service, or civilian displacement) or Option B (researching a Nova Scotia soldier via Canadian Letters or Veterans Affairs), use this guide to identify:
• Which war / mission was it? Check the dates and locations on Page 1.
• Why did Canada go? Was it an Empire duty, a UN defense, or a NATO mission?
• What was their role? Combat infantry, navy convoy, mechanic, nurse, or home front worker?
• The human reality: Did they return home? How did the experience shape their family?
