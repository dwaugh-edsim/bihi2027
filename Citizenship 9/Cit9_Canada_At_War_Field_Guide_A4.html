THE FOUR MAJOR WARS COMMON KNOWLEDGE BASE WITH THEATER MAPS
1. World War I 1914 to 1918 ~620,000 SERVED • ~66,000 KILLED
What the war was actually about: Rival European empires spent decades building giant armies and secret alliances like a line of dominoes. When a Serbian nationalist assassinated the Austrian Archduke in 1914, the dominoes fell and dragged dozens of countries into four years of slaughter in the mud. Canada was a British colony with no separate foreign policy, so when Britain declared war, all Canadians were automatically at war too.
Where: Western Front trenches across Belgium and northern France.
Enemies: German Empire and Austro-Hungarian Empire.
End: Allied breakthrough in 1918; Armistice signed November 11.
Nova Scotia hook: Halifax was the primary troop departure port. On Dec 6, 1917, the munitions ship Mont-Blanc exploded, killing nearly 2,000 civilians.
WESTERN FRONT: FRANCE & BELGIUM
2. World War II 1939 to 1945 ~1,100,000 SERVED • ~45,000 KILLED
What the war was actually about: The seeds of WWII were planted in 1919 when the Treaty of Versailles forced Germany to take total blame for WWI and pay crushing financial penalties (reparations), ruining its economy and humiliating the German people. Adolf Hitler exploited twenty years of bitter German resentment and poverty to seize power, promising revenge and world conquest. Joined by imperial Japan, Nazi Germany invaded its neighbours and murdered six million Jewish people in the Holocaust. This time, an independent Canada debated in Parliament and voted to enter the war on our own.
Where: Atlantic Ocean, Europe (Italy, Normandy D-Day, Netherlands), Pacific.
Enemies: The Axis Powers (Nazi Germany, Fascist Italy, Imperial Japan).
End: Germany surrendered May 1945; Japan surrendered August 1945.
Nova Scotia hook: Halifax was the Allied Convoy Capital. Merchant ships assembled in Bedford Basin under constant German U-boat threat.
NORTH ATLANTIC CONVOY & EUROPE
3. The Korean War 1950 to 1953 ~26,000 SERVED • 516 KILLED
What the war was actually about: During the Cold War, the world was split between the Communist bloc (Soviet Union and China) and Western democracies (US, Canada, and allies). Korea was divided into a Communist North and a pro-Western South. In June 1950, the North launched a surprise invasion that almost pushed the South into the sea. The United Nations sent troops to stop the takeover. After three brutal years in freezing mountain trenches, the war ended in a stalemate.
Where: Korean Peninsula, rugged mountain hills along the 38th Parallel.
Enemies: North Korean communist forces and Chinese army divisions.
End: Armistice signed July 27, 1953; DMZ still divides North and South today.
Legacy: Known as "The Forgotten War", but Canadian troops saw vicious combat at Kapyong (Hill 677).
KOREA: 38TH PARALLEL STALEMATE
4. The War in Afghanistan 2001 to 2014 ~40,000 SERVED • 158 SOLDIERS KILLED
What the war was actually about: On September 11, 2001, Al-Qaeda terrorists hijacked four airliners and attacked New York and Washington, killing nearly 3,000 civilians, including 24 Canadians. Al-Qaeda was based in Afghanistan under the protection of the Taliban government. Canada joined NATO allies under Article 5 to remove the Taliban from power and prevent future attacks. Canadian troops spent over a decade fighting insurgents in the dangerous dust of Kandahar.
Where: Kandahar Province, baking deserts, grape rows, and mountain valleys.
Enemies: Taliban insurgent fighters and roadside bomb (IED) networks.
End: Combat mission ended 2011; training mission finished 2014.
Nova Scotia hook: Pte. Nathan Smith of Tatamagouche was killed at Tarnak Farm in 2002: one of Canada's first combat deaths since Korea.
AFGHANISTAN: KANDAHAR PROVINCE
WHO SERVED IN THE SHADOWS? CITIZENSHIP AND RECOGNITION

No. 2 Construction Battalion (Nova Scotia's Black Battalion)

In 1914, Black volunteers were turned away at recruiting offices by white officers. Black leaders fought back, demanding the right to serve. In 1916, Canada created the No. 2 Construction Battalion, based in Pictou and Halifax. Over 600 Black men served in France, harvesting timber and building supply railways under artillery fire. In 2022, the Prime Minister delivered an official national apology in Truro for the anti-Black racism they faced.

Indigenous Veterans: Service Without Equal Rights

Over 7,000 First Nations, Inuit, and Métis soldiers volunteered in WWI and WWII, serving as decorated snipers and code talkers (like Francis Pegahmagabow and Tommy Prince). Yet when they returned home, they discovered they were still denied the right to vote in federal elections until 1960, could not enter Legion halls, and were denied equal veterans' benefits and land grants under the federal Indian Act.

CANADA AS A PEACEKEEPER FOUR ESSENTIAL MISSIONS WITH LOCATION MAPS

1. Suez Crisis (1956): The Birth of Peacekeeping

The Crisis: Britain, France, and Israel invaded Egypt to seize the Suez Canal. The Soviets threatened nuclear war.

The Move: Diplomat Lester B. Pearson proposed neutral Blue Helmets to separate the armies so invaders could pull out. Pearson won the Nobel Peace Prize.

SUEZ CANAL & SINAI

2. Cyprus (1964 to Present): The Green Line

The Crisis: Fighting exploded between Greek and Turkish Cypriots on the Mediterranean island of Cyprus.

The Reality: Canadians guarded the buffer zone. When Turkey invaded in 1974, Canadians defended Nicosia Airport under fire. 28 Canadians died holding the line.

CYPRUS: THE GREEN LINE

3. The Balkans (1992 to 1995): Medak Pocket

The Crisis: Yugoslavia collapsed into ethnic civil war. Canadian peacekeepers protected civilians from snipers and shells.

Medak Pocket (1993): 2nd Battalion PPCLI fought a 14-hour pitched battle against Croatian forces to stop a massacre. Canada's biggest battle since Korea.

BOSNIA & MEDAK POCKET

4. Rwanda (1994): The Limit of Peacekeeping

The Crisis: Extremists launched a genocide against the Tutsi minority, murdering roughly 800,000 people in 100 days.

The Moral Stand: Gen. Roméo Dallaire begged for troops to stop it. The UN refused and ordered him to leave. Dallaire stayed and saved thousands, but the world looked away.

RWANDA: CENTRAL AFRICA
HOME FRONT NOVA SCOTIA WHEN THE WAR REACHED OUR DOORSTEP
HALIFAX HARBOUR & BASIN
Halifax Explosion (Dec 6, 1917) French munitions ship Mont-Blanc carrying 2,900 tonnes of explosives collided with Imo in the Narrows. Wiped out the North End, killed 2,000, injured 9,000. Deadliest disaster in Canadian history.
Battle of the Atlantic (1939 to 1945) Halifax was the lifeline convoy port feeding Britain. German U-boats prowled offshore, sinking merchant ships and the passenger ferry SS Caribou in Cabot Strait (137 killed). Mandatory city blackouts.
The VE Day Riots (May 7 to 8, 1945) Overcrowded city, rationing, and closed liquor stores caused 10,000 sailors and citizens to riot upon victory in Europe. Downtown storefronts smashed and looted along Barrington and Gottingen streets.
Bedford Magazine Explosion (July 1945) Just 10 weeks after VE Day, a fire at Magazine Hill jetty detonated depth charges and torpedoes for 24 hours. City evacuated; heroic fire crews saved the main bunker from leveling the harbour.

Connecting to Your 300-Word Report (Google Classroom):
Whether you choose Option A (interviewing family about someone who lived through war, displacement, or home front work) or Option B (researching a Nova Scotia soldier via Canadian Letters or Veterans Affairs), answer these four anchors:
• Which war or mission was it? Check dates and theater maps on Page 1.
• Why did Canada go? Was it an Empire duty, UN defense, or NATO alliance?
• What was their role? Infantry, nurse, bomber mechanic, or home front?
• The human reality: Did they return home? How did it affect their family?
