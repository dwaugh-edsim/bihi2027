Citizenship 9 • Term 2 • Country Profile Project

Research Process Guide

How to take a country and a global issue and turn them into a 6-slide, fully sourced project. Keep this open in one tab while your deck lives in another.

Outcome (verbatim from the curriculum)

Evaluate the consequences of action and inaction as twenty-first century global citizens.

0References: what they are and why every slide needs them

Referencing means naming the sources your information came from. It is not decoration; it is part of the mark (citation rigor is one of the five criteria). Four honest reasons:

  • Show your work: citations prove you researched instead of inventing.
  • Give credit: the people who did the work get named.
  • Let readers check you: anyone can follow your trail and see whether the claim holds.
  • Build the habit: every course you ever take after grade 9 will demand this. Learn it once, cheaply, now.

Anatomy of an APA reference

Author or organization. (Year). Title. Site or publisher. URL

Environment and Climate Change Canada. (2022). Canada's National Adaptation Strategy. https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change.html
"climate change is bad and countries need to act" - some blog, no author, no date

Use a citation generator so formatting is not your problem: MyBib, Scribbr, or BibGuru (all free). Paste the URL, choose APA 7, copy the result, check it looks like the good example above. One credit line per image you did not take ("Photo: creator, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA") is enough for pictures.

1Get oriented (before any deep research)

  • Find your country on a map. Continent, neighbours, ocean.
  • Read the Wikipedia introduction twice. It exists to orient you.
  • Know three things cold: where it is, what language(s), what kind of government.
You do not need to memorize everything

Slide 2 needs 3-4 sentences. Anything more is Stage 2 work dressed up as background.

2Understand the issue itself

The classic mistake

"Climate change" is not a project. It is half a topic. Your project is [country]'s response to [specific piece of the issue]. "Pakistan's response to catastrophic flooding" is a project. Same issue, smaller door.

  • What exactly is the problem, with one real number?
  • Why does it cross borders? (Money, weather, refugees, supply chains, oceans, viruses: something must cross.)
  • Why does THIS country feel it differently than most?

3Research the response: what has the government DONE?

This is the core of the project. Where to dig:

  • Government sites: search "[country] government [issue] policy".
  • News: "[country] [issue] response 2024" or 2025 (recency matters).
  • UN and NGOs: UNHCR, UN OCHA, WHO, Amnesty, IOM: they monitor country responses professionally.
  • Treaties and pledges: climate = "[country] NDC"; refugees = "[country] UNHCR"; rights = "[country] OHCHR".
Promises are not actions

Politicians announce; follow-through varies. For every plan you find, ask: did it pass, did it get funded, did enforcement happen? Your Slide 4 separates what they SAID from what they DID.

4Map the consequences

For every action or inaction, run five questions:

  • Intended: was this the result they wanted?
  • Unintended: what happened that nobody planned?
  • Short-term: what is happening now or soon?
  • Long-term: does this matter in 10+ years?
  • Who carries it: citizens, environment, economy, neighbours?

5Research Canada on the same issue

  • What has Canada actually DONE? (Search "Canada [issue] policy" and "Global Affairs Canada [issue]".)
  • Is the approach similar or different from your country's?
  • Why the difference? Money, geography, politics, distance from the problem?
Double duty

Your Canada's Response Brief (Week 2-3) IS this research. Do it well once and Slide 6 is already built.

6Check every source before you use it

  • Who wrote it: a name, or an organization you have heard of?
  • When: is it recent enough to still be true?
  • Why did they write it: informing you, or selling you something?

8Now build the slides

  • One idea per slide. If a slide needs "and also", it is two slides.
  • Bullets, not sentences. The audience reads AND listens; short bullets let both happen.
  • Visuals earn their place: maps, photos, one chart beat decoration.
  • Cite as you go. The builder on the project page takes 30 seconds per source.

Pitfalls that cost marks every year

  • Background bloat: two slides of geography, one line of response.
  • Promise-ology: quoting pledges without checking follow-through.
  • Wikipedia cited as a final source instead of mined for real ones.
  • Slides without citations, "sources are on the last slide" is not the deal; 2+ go on the bottom of each slide.
  • Template junk: leftover slides from a design template you grabbed (this actually happened; delete them).
  • Reading your slides aloud word for word: know them, look up.
