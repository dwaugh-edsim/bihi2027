Answer 1 • The United Nations

The club of almost every country on Earth

1945
founded, right after WWII
193
member countries today
1945
Canada: a founding member
  • The UN's job in three verbs: keep peace, protect rights, deliver aid.
  • It was built by countries who had just watched WWII destroy tens of millions of lives, on one idea: talk before you fight, and agree on rules all countries share.
  • Headquarters: New York City. Not a world government. It has power only because countries agree to give it some.

The UN • Who actually decides?

Two rooms, two very different powers

General Assembly • 193 seats
  • Every member country, one vote each. Canada's vote counts exactly as much as Tuvalu's.
  • Passes resolutions: the world's opinion, officially worded.
  • Cannot force countries to act.
Security Council • 15 seats
  • Five permanent members with a veto: USA, UK, France, Russia, China.
  • Ten elected members rotate through.
  • Its decisions ARE binding: peacekeeping missions, sanctions.

The catch: one veto can block action even when every other country agrees. Remember this when your topic asks "what did the international response actually do?"

Answer 2 • Human rights

Rights you have because you are human

  • Not earned. Not a reward for good behaviour. Not a gift from governments.
  • You have them at birth; governments exist (partly) to protect them.
  • They are universal: the same list applies everywhere on Earth, to everyone.
  • The hard question your project will keep hitting: if the government is the one violating rights, who steps in?

Human rights • The document

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948)

1948
adopted, three years after WWII
30
articles
500+
languages: the world's most translated document
  • Written as a direct answer to the Holocaust and the war: the world wrote down, for the first time, what every human being is owed.
  • Drafting committee chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt.
  • Honesty note: the UDHR is a declaration, not a treaty. It is not enforceable law by itself. It is the standard the world agreed to measure countries against.

UDHR • The articles you will actually use

Seven articles, plain words

Article 1

Born free and equal in dignity and rights.

Article 2

No discrimination: the list applies to everyone, no exceptions for race, sex, language, religion, or anything else.

Article 3

Life, liberty, and security of person.

Article 5

No torture, ever, no matter what.

Article 14

The right to flee and seek safety in another country (asylum).

Article 19

Speak, read, and know: freedom of opinion and information.

Article 26

Education, free at least in the early years. The reason girls' education bans in Afghanistan break this article.

Answer 3 (part A) • Enforcement at the UN

How do rights actually get enforced?

  • Treaties: countries sign promises. The big one for you is the Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989): every UN member has ratified it except one, the United States.
  • Review committees: countries must file reports and get questioned on their records. Public, written down, embarrassing if bad.
  • Courts: the International Court of Justice settles disputes between countries (remember Vanuatu's climate case: that is what students your age started there).
  • The honest truth: the UN cannot storm a country. Its real power is pressure: reports, public shame, sanctions, aid conditions, courts. Whether pressure works is exactly what your project argues.
SOURCES ON THIS SLIDE: UNICEF. (1989). Convention on the Rights of the Child. https://www.unicef.org/child-rights-convention • ICJ. (2025). Climate advisory opinion.

Answer 3 (part B) • Canada's answer

The Charter of Rights and Freedoms (1982)

  • Part of the Constitution Act: the highest law in Canada. Any other law that breaks it can be struck down by the courts.
  • Applies to government action: Parliament, police, schools, public institutions. It does not referee private arguments between people.
  • You just finished a unit on citizenship rights, from the Peace and Friendship treaties to Donald Marshall Jr. The Charter is the modern machinery those cases ran through: Marshall's fishing rights were a treaties question, decided through the courts the Charter system built on.
SOURCES ON THIS SLIDE: Government of Canada. (1982). Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms. https://canada.ca/charter

Charter • The sections you will actually use

Five sections, plain words

Section 2 • Fundamental freedoms

Conscience, religion, thought, expression, peaceful assembly, association.

Section 7 • Life and security

Life, liberty, and security of the person: no government taking them away except in line with justice.

Section 15 • Equality

Equal treatment and protection under the law, without discrimination.

Section 1 • Reasonable limits

Rights in Canada are not absolute: limits must be "demonstrably justified in a free and democratic society". Your right to swing your fist stops at someone's face.

Section 33 • Notwithstanding clause

A government can override sections 2, 7, and 15 for five years at a time. The escape hatch. Quebec has used it for language laws.

SOURCES ON THIS SLIDE: Government of Canada. (1982). Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms, sections 1-33. https://canada.ca/charter

Put them side by side

Same idea, very different teeth

UDHR • The world's standard
  • Applies to every human, everywhere.
  • No court of its own. Power = pressure.
  • Countries use it to judge each other (and to hide from judgment).
Charter • Canadian law
  • Applies in Canada, to government action.
  • Real teeth: courts enforce it, laws get struck down.
  • Even it can be limited (s.1) or overridden (s.33).

Your project lives between the two: does the country's action match the promise? That is the outcome, word for word: evaluate the consequences of action and inaction.
