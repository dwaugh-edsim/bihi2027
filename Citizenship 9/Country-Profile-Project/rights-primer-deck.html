  • The UN's job in three verbs: keep peace, protect rights, deliver aid.
  • Built right after WWII destroyed tens of millions of lives, around one idea: talk before you fight, and agree on rules all countries share.

The UN • Who actually decides? 4 / 13

Two rooms, two very different powers

General Assembly • 193 seats
  • Every member country gets one vote. Canada's vote counts the exact same as Tuvalu's.
  • Passes resolutions: official statements of the world's opinion.
  • Cannot force any country to obey.
Security Council • 15 seats
  • Five permanent members hold a veto (a single "no" vote that kills any decision): USA, UK, France, Russia, China.
  • Ten other countries rotate in for two-year terms.
  • Its decisions are binding (legally required): peacekeeping missions and sanctions (trade or money penalties).

The catch: one veto can block action even when almost every other country agrees. Remember this when your project asks "what did the international response actually do?"

Answer 2 • Human rights 5 / 13

Rights you have because you are human

  • Not earned. Not a reward for good behaviour. Not a gift from a government.
  • You have them at birth; governments exist (in part) to protect them.
  • They are universal: the same list applies to every person everywhere on Earth.
  • The hard question your project will keep hitting: if the government itself is violating rights, who steps in?
Human rights • The document 6 / 13

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948)

1948
adopted 3 years after WWII
30
articles
500+
languages: most translated document on Earth
  • Written directly in response to the Holocaust and WWII: for the first time, the world wrote down what every human being is owed.
  • Drafting committee chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt.
  • Honesty note: the UDHR is a declaration (a shared statement of values), not a treaty (a legally binding contract). It has no police force of its own; it is the standard the world measures countries against.

UDHR • The articles you will actually use 7 / 13

Seven articles, plain words

Article 1

Born free and equal in dignity and rights.

Article 2

No discrimination: the whole list applies to everyone, regardless of race, sex, language, religion, or background.

Article 3

Life, liberty, and security of person (freedom from harm, threats, or unfair arrest).

Article 5

No torture or cruel punishment, ever.

Article 14

The right to flee danger and seek safety in another country (asylum).

Article 19

Speak, read, and know: freedom of opinion, expression, and information.

Article 26

Right to education, free at least in the early years. Girls' education bans in Afghanistan directly break this article.

Answer 3 (part A) • Enforcement at the UN 8 / 13

How do rights actually get enforced?

  • Treaties: formal agreements countries sign and ratify (officially approve into law). Example: the Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989), ratified by every UN member except the United States.
  • Review committees: countries must file public reports and answer tough questions on their record.
  • International courts: the International Court of Justice (the UN's highest court in the Netherlands) settles legal disputes between countries (including Vanuatu's student-led climate case).
  • The honest truth: the UN cannot simply invade a country. Its real power is pressure: public reports, sanctions, aid conditions, and court rulings. Whether pressure works is exactly what your project evaluates.
SOURCES ON THIS SLIDE:UNICEF. (1989). Convention on the Rights of the Child. https://www.unicef.org/child-rights-convention • ICJ. (2025). Climate advisory opinion.
Answer 3 (part B) • Canada's answer 9 / 13

The Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms (1982)

  • Part of Canada's Constitution: the highest law in Canada. Any federal or provincial law that breaks it can be struck down by judges.
  • Applies to government action: Parliament, police, public schools, and courts. It does not settle private arguments between neighbours.
  • Think back to our unit on treaties and Donald Marshall Jr.: the modern Canadian court system and Constitution (including Indigenous and treaty rights in s. 35) are what let people challenge government unfairness and win.
SOURCES ON THIS SLIDE:Government of Canada. (1982). Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms. https://canada.ca/charter
Charter • The sections you will actually use 10 / 13

Five sections, plain words

Section 2 • Fundamental freedoms

Conscience, religion, thought, expression, peaceful protest (assembly), and joining groups or unions (association).

Section 7 • Life and security

Life, liberty, and personal safety: the government cannot take them away except through fair legal rules.

Section 15 • Equality

Equal treatment and equal protection under the law, without discrimination.

Section 1 • Reasonable limits

Rights in Canada are not unlimited: the government can limit a right if it proves the limit is fair and necessary in a democracy (for example, laws against hate speech).

Section 33 • Notwithstanding clause

The temporary override: a federal or provincial government can pass a law that pauses s. 2, 7, or 15 for up to 5 years at a time (used in Quebec for language laws).

SOURCES ON THIS SLIDE:Government of Canada. (1982). Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms, sections 1-33. https://canada.ca/charter
Put them side by side 11 / 13

Same idea, very different teeth

UDHR • The world's standard
  • Applies to every human, everywhere on Earth.
  • No court or police of its own. Power = international pressure.
  • Countries use it to hold each other accountable (or ignore it when convenient).
Charter • Canadian law
  • Applies inside Canada, to government actions and laws.
  • Real legal teeth: Canadian courts enforce it and strike down unfair laws.
  • Even here, rights can be reasonably limited (s. 1) or temporarily overridden (s. 33).

Your project lives in the gap between the two: does the country's action match what it promised? That is the curriculum outcome: evaluate the consequences of action and inaction.
