### SOURCE 1: The Murder & Wrongful Conviction (1971)

**Title:** Beat 1: The Midnight Walk in Wentworth Park  
**Focus:** The Crime, The Frame-Up, and 11 Stolen Years  

On midnight of May 28, 1971, two seventeen-year-old boys were walking through Wentworth Park in Sydney, Nova Scotia. One was Sandy Seale, a Black youth. The other was Donald Marshall Jr., a Mi'kmaw teenager from Membertou First Nation.

In the dark, they crossed paths with an eccentric, violent 58-year-old man named Roy Ebsary, who was carrying a concealed knife. Without warning, Ebsary lunged at them. Seale was fatally stabbed in the stomach. Marshall was slashed across the arm while trying to defend his friend.

Donald did what any panicked seventeen-year-old would do: he sprinted directly to the Sydney police station to get an ambulance and report the killer. But instead of sending patrol cars to hunt for Roy Ebsary, Sydney police detectives did something shocking. They locked onto Donald Marshall Jr.

Within hours, the reporting witness became the prime murder suspect. Sydney police developed extreme "tunnel vision." Deep-rooted racism against Indigenous people led detectives to believe a Mi'kmaw teen was naturally violent. Police never questioned Roy Ebsary that night. Instead, detectives interrogated teenage bystanders, threatening them until they signed false statements claiming they saw Donald hold the knife.

Donald faced an all-white jury and an all-white courtroom. His court-appointed defence lawyer never interviewed witnesses or challenged police stories. The jury took just minutes to convict seventeen-year-old Donald Marshall Jr. of non-capital murder. He was sentenced to life in prison.

Donald spent eleven of his prime years—from age 17 to 28—inside maximum-security federal penitentiaries at Dorchester and Springhill, surviving violence and solitary confinement while insisting on his innocence. The real killer walked free on the streets of Nova Scotia for over a decade.

**Focus Question for the Class:**  
*What caused police to target the reporting witness instead of hunting the real killer, and what does this show about how racial bias can corrupt an investigation?*

### SOURCE 2: The Marshall Inquiry (1989)

**Title:** Beat 2: The Royal Commission on the Donald Marshall Jr. Prosecution  
**Focus:** Exoneration, Systemic Racism Exposed, and Institutional Reform  

Donald Marshall Jr.'s nightmare finally began to unravel in 1982. A key witness came forward to admit police had coerced him into lying, and Roy Ebsary's own daughter went to authorities after hearing her father brag about the stabbing. Donald was released on bail after eleven stolen years, and in 1983, his conviction was officially overturned.

But the justice system didn't want to admit its failure. The Nova Scotia Court of Appeal shockingly blamed Donald for his own wrongful conviction, claiming he was "the author of his own misfortune."

Public outrage forced the government to establish the **Royal Commission on the Donald Marshall Jr. Prosecution**—the landmark 1989 Marshall Inquiry.

For three years, the Commission subpoenaed police officers, prosecutors, judges, and politicians. Its final report sent shockwaves across Canada:
1. **Total Exoneration:** Donald Marshall Jr. was completely innocent.
2. **Systemic Racism:** The Commission declared that racism against Indigenous people infected the justice system at every level—from the initial Sydney police detectives, to the Crown prosecutors, to the trial judge, all the way to the Court of Appeal.
3. **The Damning Verdict:** *"The justice system failed Donald Marshall Jr. at every step."*

The Marshall Inquiry forced a massive reckoning across Canada. It led to Nova Scotia creating the first independent Public Prosecution Service in Canada (to stop police and politicians from controlling charges), created Indigenous court worker programs, and forced Canadian courts to confront over-incarceration—directly paving the way for the 1999 **Gladue Principle**, which mandates that judges must account for systemic discrimination before sentencing Indigenous people.

Donald was awarded compensation, but the prime of his youth could never be returned.

**Focus Question for the Class:**  
*Why did the Royal Commission conclude that the justice system failed Donald Marshall Jr. "at every step", and what concrete reforms came out of this inquiry?*

### SOURCE 3: The Eels — Laws vs. Treaties (1993)

**Title:** Beat 3: Pomquet Harbour — Modern Fisheries Act vs. 1760 Treaty  
**Focus:** The 463 lbs of Eels, The Ambush, and The Constitutional Clash  

In August 1993, Donald Marshall Jr. was out on the quiet waters of Pomquet Harbour in Antigonish County with his partner Jane McMillan. Over two days, they harvested 463 pounds of adult eels using a traditional fyke net. 

Donald loaded the eels into tubs and sold them to a commercial buyer for **$787.10**, using the money to pay for rent and groceries.

Moments later, armed patrol officers from the federal Department of Fisheries and Oceans (DFO) swooped in. They seized Donald’s boat, his nets, and his catch, slapping him with three federal criminal charges under Canada's *Fisheries Act*:
1. Fishing for eels out of commercial season.
2. Fishing without a federal commercial licence.
3. Fishing with an unapproved net.

To the federal government, this was an open-and-shut case. The Crown prosecutor argued: *"Canada's Fisheries Act applies to all waters and all citizens equally. No one has a special right to fish without a permit."*

Donald refused to plead guilty. Instead of fighting over the facts, Donald's defence walked into court with a 233-year-old piece of parchment: **The 1760 Treaty of Peace and Friendship**, signed between the Mi'kmaq and the British Crown.

Marshall’s lawyers pointed directly to Article 4:
> *“Saving only unto the said tribe the free liberty of Hunting & Fishing **as usual**... And that a **Truckhouse** [Crown trading post] shall be established... for furnishing the Indians with **necessaries** in Exchange for their Peltry and other commodities.”*

Marshall argued:
- The Mi'kmaq never lost a war of conquest and never surrendered or sold their land in this treaty.
- The Crown legally guaranteed that Mi'kmaw harvesters could trade their catch for survival necessities.
- In 1982, **Section 35 of the Canadian Constitution** made treaty rights supreme constitutional law.

The collision was set: Does modern federal fisheries law outrank an 18th-century treaty covenant?

**Focus Question for the Class:**  
*Which argument is legally stronger: The Crown's argument that one fisheries law must apply equally to everyone, or Marshall's argument that constitutional treaty promises outrank federal regulations?*

### SOURCE 4: The 1999 Legal Decision (R. v. Marshall)

**Title:** Beat 4: Supreme Court Acquittal & The "Moderate Livelihood" Trap  
**Focus:** The Historic Section 35 Victory and the Fatal Flaw of Ambiguity  

On September 17, 1999, the Supreme Court of Canada delivered its historic decision in ***R. v. Marshall***. 

Against all odds, Donald Marshall Jr. won. The highest court in Canada **acquitted him of all criminal charges**.

Justice Ian Binnie, writing for the majority, ruled that the 1760 Treaty was not a dusty museum piece. Under Section 35 of the Constitution, the treaty was alive, binding, and supreme. The Court confirmed that the 1760 Truckhouse clause gave the Mi'kmaq an ongoing constitutional right to harvest and sell fish to secure a **"moderate livelihood."**

The Court defined a moderate livelihood as:
*Securing the basic necessities of life—food, clothing, housing, and basic amenities—for individual families and communities.*

It was the first time in Canadian history that an Indigenous commercial right to harvest and trade was recognized under the Constitution. 

**However, the ruling contained one fatal flaw:**  
The Supreme Court never defined what a "moderate livelihood" actually meant in dollars, boat limits, or calendar seasons! Is it $30,000 a year? $60,000? $100,000? Could Indigenous fishers harvest in the summer when non-Indigenous commercial seasons were closed? 

The judges explicitly told the federal government and First Nations: *Go negotiate the rules together.*

Instead, the federal government stalled. Ottawa failed to negotiate a regulatory framework, leaving the definition of "moderate livelihood" completely blank.

**Focus Question for the Class:**  
*Why was the 1999 Marshall decision a historic victory for Indigenous rights, but why did leaving the definition of "moderate livelihood" blank create a legal time-bomb?*

### SOURCE 5: Modern Consequences (The Two "So Whats")

**Title:** Beat 5: From 1760 to Today — Lobster Riots & Sovereign Cannabis  
**Focus:** The 2020 St. Mary's Bay Dispute and 2025–26 Sovereign Cannabis Raids  

Because the federal government left "moderate livelihood" undefined for decades, the legal vacuum exploded into conflict right here in Nova Scotia.

#### Modern Consequence #1: The 2020 St. Mary's Bay Lobster Riots
In September 2020—twenty-one years after the Marshall decision—**Sipekne'katik First Nation** decided they had waited long enough. Exercising their 1760 treaty right, they launched their own self-regulated lobster fishery in St. Mary’s Bay outside the federal DFO season.

The backlash was violent. Non-Indigenous commercial fishers blockaded the wharf at Saulnierville, fired flares at Indigenous boats, cut hundreds of traps, and surrounded Mi'kmaw fishers. Days later, a commercial lobster pound holding Mi'kmaw catch in Middle West Pubnico was burned completely to the ground.
- Commercial fishers claimed: *"Conservation! Everyone must follow one federal season."*
- Mi'kmaw fishers replied: *"Sipekne'katik only set 250 traps—commercial fleets set over 350,000! This is about monopoly control, not conservation."*

#### Modern Consequence #2: The 2025–26 Sovereign Cannabis Raids
Drive through communities like Millbrook or Sipekne'katik today, and you will see dozens of sovereign cannabis dispensaries flying the Mi'kmaw Grand Council flag. 

The owners don't buy provincial tax stamps or hold Nova Scotia Liquor Corporation licences. They argue: *Under the 1760 Treaty, we have a constitutional right to trade "commodities" on unceded land to support our families—cannabis is just the modern equivalent of eels.*

The Province and RCMP disagree, conducting tactical raids, seizing millions in cash and product under Canada's *Cannabis Act*. Storefronts reopen within days, setting up the next major constitutional showdown at the Supreme Court.

#### The Big Takeaway: "We Are All Treaty People"
The peace treaties of 1760 did not give "special privileges" to Indigenous people—they are the legal agreements that allow non-Indigenous Canadians to live, work, and own land in Nova Scotia peacefully. When governments fail to honor treaty covenants, peace on the water and land breaks down.

**Focus Question for the Class:**  
*How do both the 2020 lobster clashes and the 2025–26 cannabis raids trace directly back to Donald Marshall Jr., and what does it mean to say "We Are All Treaty People"?*
