## 1. Fast Timeline: Europeans Arrive in Mi'kma'ki
Long before Europeans arrived, Mi'kmaq people lived throughout Mi'kma'ki—an ancestral territory that includes all of present-day Nova Scotia, Prince Edward Island, much of New Brunswick, the Gaspé peninsula in Quebec, and parts of Newfoundland. 

Mi'kmaq society was organized into seven traditional districts (including *Sipekne'katik*, *Kespukwitk*, and *Unama'kik*). It had its own legal systems, Grand Council (*Santé Mawio'mi*), seasonal economies, and diplomatic alliances.

European contact began primarily through the North Atlantic cod fishery and seasonal fur trade. French, Basque, Portuguese, and English crews arrived for seasonal work, followed by permanent trading posts and settlements like Port-Royal (1605) and Halifax (1749). The Mi'kmaq were active geopolitical negotiators, forming alliances (especially with the French) to protect their territorial independence.

---

## 2. Contact Had Real Consequences
European arrival brought severe, irreversible pressures to Mi'kma'ki:
- **Epidemic Disease:** Diseases brought from Europe devastated Indigenous populations who had no biological immunity.
- **Ecological Competition:** Settler clear-cutting, farming, and coastal occupation disrupted traditional seasonal hunting, fishing, and travel routes.
- **Imperial Warfare:** Britain and France fought global wars for imperial dominance, using Mi'kma'ki as a battleground and drawing Indigenous nations into military treaties.
- **Power Asymmetries:** Over the 18th century, British naval power, permanent fortifications (like the Halifax Citadel), and military garrisons created a profound imbalance of power.

## 3. The Peace and Friendship Treaties (1725–1779)
Between 1725 and 1779, the British Crown and the Mi'kmaq, Wolastoqey, and Peskotomuhkati signed a sequence of agreements known collectively as the **Peace and Friendship Treaties**. The two most legally significant treaties in Nova Scotia are:
1. **The 1752 Treaty** (Governor Peregrine Hopson and Chief Jean-Baptiste Cope).
2. **The 1760–1761 Treaties** (signed following the fall of Louisbourg and Quebec).

### The Crucial Principle: NO CESSION OF LAND
Unlike the later "Numbered Treaties" in Western Canada, **the Peace and Friendship Treaties never surrendered, sold, or ceded one acre of land**. 
- They were treaties of mutual peace, friendship, and regulated trade between distinct nations.
- The Crown agreed that the Mi'kmaq would not be hindered in hunting, fishing, and harvesting *"as usual."*
- They established that both peoples would live side-by-side under a framework of mutual peace.

### Were the Mi'kmaq free to sign?
This is the question to sit with. Mi'kmaw leaders were not simply handed "no choice," but they negotiated under real pressure: European diseases had hurt their communities, British military power kept growing, and old trade networks were shifting. At the same time, the British *needed* Mi'kmaq cooperation — especially during their wars with France. So the treaties were signings between parties with very unequal power. And because the agreements were written in English legal language, what Mi'kmaw negotiators understood through oral translation and ceremony may not have matched every written clause exactly.

## 4. Worldview Clash: Netukulimk vs. Crown Regulation (Docket Box 2)
To understand modern treaty disputes, students must understand two fundamentally different worldviews regarding the natural world:

### Mi'kmaw Worldview: Netukulimk & Msit No'kmaq
- **Netukulimk** is the traditional Mi'kmaw concept of taking only what you need for physical, economic, and cultural sustenance, while ensuring that the integrity and abundance of creation is preserved for future generations.
- It is guided by **Msit No'kmaq** (*"all my relations"*), the belief that humans, animals, fish, forests, and waters are interconnected and carry mutual obligations of respect.
- Harvesting is seen as an inherent birthright and a sacred stewardship responsibility, not a permission granted by an emperor or government.

### British / Colonial Worldview: State Ownership & Quotas
- Nature is categorized as property, commodities, and capital to be exploited for market profit.
- The Crown asserts absolute sovereign control over waters and land.
- Citizens only have the right to fish or harvest if the government issues an official permit, quota, or commercial license.

## 5. The Truckhouse Clause & The Marshall Decision (Docket Box 3)
In the **1760–1761 Treaties**, the British included what is known as the **Truckhouse Clause**:
> *"And that a Truckhouse shall be established at Fort Edward or elsewhere, for furnishing the Indians with necessaries in Exchange for their Peltry and other commodities."*

The British intended this clause to monopolize trade and cut off French merchants. But in doing so, the Crown legally guaranteed that the Mi'kmaq had the right to trade goods to secure their **"necessaries"** (food, shelter, basic livelihood).

### 239 Years Later: R. v. Marshall (1999)
In 1993, Donald Marshall Jr. caught and sold 463 pounds of eels in Pomquet Harbour without a federal Department of Fisheries and Oceans (DFO) commercial licence. He was charged with illegal fishing.

Marshall took his case all the way to the Supreme Court of Canada. In September 1999, the Supreme Court **acquitted Donald Marshall Jr.**, ruling that:
1. The 1760 Peace and Friendship Treaty is a valid, living constitutional document protected by **Section 35** of the *Constitution Act, 1982*.
2. The Truckhouse Clause guaranteed a modern right to fish, hunt, and gather to earn a **moderate livelihood** (basic sustenance and reasonable living standards), even outside federal commercial licensing.

### The Modern Conflict: St. Mary's Bay (2020)
In September 2020, Sipekne'katik First Nation launched a self-regulated lobster fishery in St. Mary's Bay under their treaty rights, issuing their own community tags outside the federal DFO season. Commercial non-Indigenous fishers protested, leading to blockades and violence. 

The core debate was jurisdictional: **Does the federal government have the right to regulate treaty fisheries under the claim of conservation, or do Mi'kmaw communities have the inherent right to govern their own harvest under Netukulimk?**

## 6. Two Hard Questions About Reading the Treaties (Trial Evidence Rounds 3 & 4)

### Written Text vs. Oral Tradition
The treaties survive on paper as written colonial documents, but the Mi'kmaw side carries them as oral history — promises remembered through ceremony and passed down by Elders. Mi'kmaw understanding includes the *spirit and intent* of the agreement, not just its literal words. Courts today increasingly accept oral history as evidence, but governments still lean on the written text to set legal limits.

### Sovereignty vs. "Submission"
Some written versions of the treaties contain language that can be read as the Mi'kmaq submitting to the British Crown. Mi'kmaw interpretation holds that these were agreements between independent nations, and that words like "submission" may reflect mistranslation or the assumptions of the British scribes who wrote them down. The British, for their part, treated the treaties as bringing Mi'kma'ki under Crown law. Which reading you accept shapes what the treaties mean in 2026.

## 7. "We Are All Treaty People" (Docket Box 4)
When Nova Scotians say *"We are all treaty people,"* it means that treaties are two-sided covenants that bind everyone living in Mi'kma'ki today:
- **For Mi'kmaq people:** Treaties recognize inherent sovereignty, rights to hunt and fish for a moderate livelihood, and stewardship of unceded land.
- **For Non-Indigenous citizens:** Treaties are the legal reason settlers and their descendants were allowed to settle and live in peace in Mi'kma'ki without war. 
- **The Civic Responsibility:** Living in peace is not passive. It requires learning the true history, respecting Section 35 constitutional rights, supporting economic reconciliation, and ensuring government policy adheres to the honour of the Crown.
