Canada at War: The Facts Every Citizen Needs to Know
1914 TO PRESENT
The Core Purpose of Remembrance

Remembrance Day is not a celebration of military glory. It is a sober recognition of human sacrifice, the terrifying cost of conflict, and our civic responsibility to protect peace. Before you research an individual soldier or family story, you need to understand where Canada fought, who we fought, and why we went.

How Canada's Role Evolved 1914 to 2014

World War I (1914): We were a British colony with no separate foreign policy. When Britain declared war, Canada was automatically at war.

World War II (1939): We were an independent nation. Our own Parliament debated for a week and voted to declare war on our own.

Korea & Peacekeeping (1950s): We fought to defend international law through the United Nations, and Lester Pearson invented the Blue Helmets.

Afghanistan (2001): We fought alongside NATO allies in collective defense after the September 11 terrorist attacks.

The Nova Scotia Footprint War at Home

Nova Scotia was never an idle spectator. Our province was a direct front line: the embarkation port for soldiers, the nerve centre for transatlantic merchant convoys, and the site of catastrophic domestic explosions and riots.

Conflict Roster • 1914–1918
1. World War I
~620,000 SERVED • ~66,000 KILLED
What was the war actually about?

Rival European empires spent decades building giant armies and secret alliances like a line of dominoes. When a Serbian nationalist assassinated the Austrian Archduke in 1914, the dominoes fell and dragged dozens of countries into four years of slaughter in the mud of the Western Front. Because Canada was a British colony with no separate foreign policy, when Britain declared war, all Canadians were automatically at war too.

The Battlefield Reality Europe

Where: Trench warfare across Belgium and northern France (Ypres, the Somme, Passchendaele, Vimy Ridge).

Who we fought: The German Empire and the Austro-Hungarian Empire.

How it ended: Allied forces broke the German line in the Hundred Days Campaign of 1918, ending with the Armistice on November 11 at 11:00 AM.

Nova Scotia Connection Halifax

The Gateway to War: Nearly every Canadian soldier sailed for Europe out of Halifax Harbour.

The Halifax Explosion (Dec 6, 1917): The war came directly to our doorstep when the munitions ship Mont-Blanc blew up in the Narrows, killing almost 2,000 people and leveling the North End.

Citizenship & Equity • WWI & WWII
Who Served in the Shadows?
EQUAL SERVICE • UNEQUAL RIGHTS
The Fight to Serve

Tens of thousands of Black and Indigenous Canadians stepped forward to fight for democracy abroad, even while their own government denied them basic civil rights at home. Their stories were omitted from schoolbooks for decades.

No. 2 Construction Battalion Pictou & Halifax

The Black Battalion: In 1914, Black volunteers were rejected at recruitment offices by white officers. Black community leaders fought back until Canada created the No. 2 Construction Battalion in 1916, based in Pictou and Halifax.

Service: Over 600 Black men served in France, harvesting timber and building supply railways under heavy shellfire.

Apology: In July 2022, the federal government delivered an official national apology in Truro, NS, for the racist treatment they endured.

Indigenous Veterans First Nations, Inuit, Métis

On the Front Lines: Over 7,000 Indigenous soldiers volunteered in WWI and WWII, serving as legendary scouts, snipers, and code talkers.

Betrayal at Home: When they returned home, they discovered they were still legally non-citizens under the Indian Act. They could not vote in federal elections until 1960, were banned from Legion halls, and were denied equal veterans' benefits and land grants.

Conflict Roster • 1939–1945
2. World War II
~1,100,000 SERVED • ~45,000 KILLED
What was the war actually about?

Adolf Hitler and Nazi Germany, joined by fascist Italy and imperial Japan, tried to conquer Europe and Asia, crushing democracies and murdering six million Jewish people in the Holocaust. This time, Canada was an independent country: Parliament debated for a week and voted to enter the war on our own. Canada converted its whole economy to fight, building ships, tanks, and bombers.

The Global Theatres Sea, Land, Air

Where: The Atlantic Ocean, Hong Kong, Italy (Ortona), France (Normandy on D-Day), and the liberation of the Netherlands.

Who we fought: The Axis Powers (Nazi Germany, Fascist Italy, Imperial Japan).

How it ended: Germany surrendered in May 1945 after Berlin fell; Japan surrendered in August 1945 after atomic bombs hit Hiroshima and Nagasaki.

Halifax: The Convoy Capital Battle of the Atlantic

The Atlantic Lifeline: Merchant ships loaded with grain, oil, and ammunition assembled in Bedford Basin before braving packs of German U-boats in the North Atlantic.

U-Boats in NS Waters: German submarines sank ships right in the Cabot Strait, including the passenger ferry SS Caribou, killing 137 people.

Home Front Nova Scotia • 1945
When War Came to Halifax: Riots and Blasts
MAY TO JULY 1945
The Pressure Cooker City

Halifax was packed with thousands of sailors and soldiers living under strict rationing and overcrowded conditions. The end of the war brought two shocking domestic crises within 10 weeks.

The VE Day Riots May 7–8, 1945

The Chaos: When victory in Europe was announced, city authorities closed restaurants and liquor stores, fearing trouble. The plan backfired.

Over 10,000 sailors, soldiers, and locals poured into downtown Halifax, looting liquor stores, smashing streetcars, and ransacking hundreds of stores along Barrington and Gottingen streets before order was restored.

Bedford Magazine Explosion July 18–19, 1945

The Second Explosion: Ten weeks after the riots, a fire ignited an ammunition jetty at Magazine Hill on the Bedford Basin.

A barge loaded with depth charges blew up, triggering 24 hours of chain-reaction explosions among 2,000 tonnes of bombs. Halifax and Dartmouth were evacuated. Courageous fire crews worked through the blasts to prevent the main underground magazine from exploding.

Conflict Roster • 1950–1953
3. The Korean War
~26,000 SERVED • 516 KILLED
What was the war actually about?

During the Cold War, the world was split between the Communist bloc (Soviet Union and China) and Western democracies (US, Canada, and allies). Korea was divided into a Communist North and a pro-Western South. In June 1950, the North launched a surprise invasion that almost pushed the South into the sea. The United Nations sent troops to stop the takeover. After three brutal years in freezing mountain trenches, the war ended in a stalemate.

The Combat Conditions Sub-Zero Trenches

Where: The mountainous Korean peninsula, particularly Hill 677 at the Battle of Kapyong.

Who we fought: North Korean communist forces and Chinese army divisions.

The Combat: Canadian soldiers fought hand-to-hand in sub-zero winter temperatures, calling in artillery strikes directly onto their own positions to stop mass infantry assaults.

How It Ended The 38th Parallel

Armistice: An armed ceasefire was signed on July 27, 1953. No peace treaty was ever signed. The border between North and South Korea remains one of the most heavily armed minefields in the world (the DMZ).

The Forgotten War: It sat quietly between the massive scale of WWII and the television coverage of Vietnam, but 516 Canadians gave their lives.

Peacekeeping Era • 1956 & 1964
Canada as a Peacekeeper: The Blue Helmets
DIPLOMACY & CEASEFIRES
The Invention of Peacekeeping

During the Cold War, small conflicts threatened to trigger nuclear war between the superpowers. Canadian diplomat Lester B. Pearson invented a radical new concept: send neutral soldiers wearing blue helmets to stand between opposing armies to enforce ceasefires without shooting.

1. Suez Crisis 1956 • Pearson's Triumph

The Flashpoint: Britain, France, and Israel launched a surprise invasion of Egypt to seize the Suez Canal. The Soviets threatened nuclear rockets.

The Solution: Pearson persuaded the United Nations to deploy the first armed peacekeeping force to separate the armies so the invaders could withdraw with dignity. Pearson won the Nobel Peace Prize.

2. Cyprus 1964 to Present • The Green Line

The Flashpoint: Violent clashes broke out between Greek Cypriots and Turkish Cypriots on the island of Cyprus. Canadians patrolled the buffer zone.

The Combat: In 1974, Turkey invaded Cyprus. Canadian peacekeepers were caught in the crossfire, holding Nicosia Airport under heavy fire. 28 Canadian peacekeepers died over three decades in Cyprus.

Peacekeeping Tested • 1990s
Peacekeeping Under Fire: Balkans and Rwanda
THE TRAGIC LIMITS
When Peacekeeping Collapsed

In the 1990s, the Cold War ended and savage ethnic civil wars erupted. Peacekeepers with light weapons found themselves trapped in sniper alleys and powerless to stop mass atrocities.

3. The Balkans / Bosnia 1992–1995 • Medak Pocket

The Civil War: Yugoslavia fractured into brutal ethnic fighting between Serbs, Croats, and Bosnians. Peacekeepers were shelled and targeted by snipers.

Medak Pocket (Sept 1993): Canadian troops (2nd Battalion PPCLI) were forced into a 14-hour firefight with Croatian army units to protect civilians: Canada's largest battle since Korea. Kept quiet by officials for years, it left soldiers with deep PTSD.

4. Rwanda 1994 • The Moral Stand

The Genocide: Extremists in Rwanda launched a slaughter of roughly 800,000 Tutsi civilians in just 100 days.

Roméo Dallaire: Canadian General Roméo Dallaire commanded the small UN force. He begged the UN for more soldiers and authority to stop the slaughter. World powers refused and ordered him to leave. Dallaire refused to abandon everyone, saving tens of thousands, but carried deep moral trauma.

Modern Combat • 2001–2014
4. The War in Afghanistan
~40,000 SERVED • 158 KILLED
What was the war actually about?

On September 11, 2001, Al-Qaeda terrorists hijacked four airliners and attacked New York and Washington, killing nearly 3,000 civilians, including 24 Canadians. Al-Qaeda was based in Afghanistan under the protection of the Taliban government. Canada joined NATO allies under Article 5 to remove the Taliban from power and prevent future attacks. Canadian troops spent over a decade fighting insurgents in the dangerous dust of Kandahar.

Modern Warfare Kandahar Province

Who we fought: Taliban insurgents, foreign fighters, and concealed improvised explosive devices (IEDs) along dirt tracks.

The Scale: Over 40,000 Canadians rotated through Afghanistan. 158 soldiers and 7 Canadian civilians were killed, and thousands suffered amputations and brain injuries.

The Highway of Heroes: The 172-km stretch of Highway 401 in Ontario where thousands of citizens lined bridges to salute fallen soldiers returning home.

Nova Scotia Connection Tatamagouche

Private Nathan Smith: Born in Tatamagouche, NS, he served with the 3rd Battalion PPCLI. On April 17, 2002, he was killed at Tarnak Farm near Kandahar in an accidental bombing: one of Canada's first combat deaths since Korea.

His mother, Charlotte Smith, served as Canada's National Silver Cross Mother, representing families of the fallen.

Your Remembrance Day Task • Room 8 Mission
What is a Veteran Today?
THE 300-WORD REPORT
Beyond the Stereotype

A veteran is not just an 80-year-old grandfather in WWII medals. A veteran in Canada today can be a 35-year-old neighbour who served in Kandahar, a sailor who patrolled the Arabian Sea, a woman combat officer, or a peacekeeper who witnessed tragedy in Bosnia.
