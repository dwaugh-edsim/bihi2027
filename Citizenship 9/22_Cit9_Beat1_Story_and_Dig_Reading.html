Part 1 · The Story of Kluskap

One telling, adapted for Grade 9. Mi'kmaw families and communities tell this story in their own ways; it is a living story, still spoken out loud in Mi'kma'ki today.

Long before any ship crossed the water, the Giver of Life, Kisu'lk, looked at the world. The land was beautiful, and it was empty. No people lived there yet.

From the red earth of the land, Kisu'lk shaped a human form. It lay in the darkness, still as stone. Then a bolt of lightning struck the earth. Its fire entered the clay, and the figure opened his eyes. He rose and looked at the stars, at the sun coming up over the water, at the land all around him. His name was Kluskap.

Kluskap walked the land, but he was alone, and being alone is hard. He asked the Giver of Life for relations.

On the rocks by the shore he met an old woman. "I am your Grandmother," she said. "I come from the stone of the earth." She taught him the old knowledge, the patience of the elders.

Later, a woman came to him from the plants of the earth, from the leaf and the dew. "I am your Mother," she said. She taught him the names of every plant, and how the plants feed us and heal us.

And then, from the foam of the waves, came a young man. "I am your Nephew," he said. He could live on the water and on the land, and he became the hunter and fisher, careful, never greedy.

The four relations lived together by the shore. Kluskap called the animals, the birds, and the fish, and gave each one a gift and a job. The eagle carried messages up to Kisu'lk. Every being had work to do, and every being was owed respect.

Then Kluskap took his relations to a place where the rivers were thick with fish and the forest was full of game. From these four relations came the Mi'kmaq, the family of this land.

And Kluskap taught them the law of the land. Today the Mi'kmaq call that law Netukulimk: take only what you need. Use all of what you take. Share with anyone who has none. Give thanks, because the plants and animals are relations, not supplies.

When his work was done, Kluskap walked into the west. Some storytellers say he rests inside his cave on Kelly's Mountain in Unama'kik, Cape Breton, where the wind goes in and out of the cliff. Some say he will return when the people need him most.

This story is not a story about the past. It is a story about belonging: who this land belongs with, and how you must live if it is your home.

Close to home

At Debert, near Cobequid Bay in Sipekne'katik district, archaeologists found a caribou-hunter camp about 13,000 years old: one of the oldest large sites in North America. The pyramids of Egypt are about 4,500.
