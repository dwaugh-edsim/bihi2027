05–10m • The Briefing

The Case Brief

One mystery: how did the first people arrive in the Americas, long before European contact?

  • For decades this looked like an open-and-shut case: one land bridge, one arrival, about 13,000 years ago.
  • New evidence has cracked the timeline wide open. People were here earlier, and deeper, than the old story allowed.
  • Your job: examine the evidence yourself. Walk the room, read the 10 case files, log your findings, and deliver a verdict.
Your Tools

10–15m • The Walk

How the Investigation Runs

About 25 minutes of walking and logging

  • Duos, in any order. Start at any case file. Most pairs begin at #01 and go around.
  • Read the card together, then log it. Talk it into an answer before anyone writes.
  • Crowded card? Skip it, loop back. Ten files, plenty of time.
  • Log as you go, not at the end. The verdict box (Q9) is the only thing that waits.

43–49m • Reveal 1

The Prime Suspect

What everyone believed for decades

  • The bridge: ice locks the water, seas drop hundreds of feet, and Beringia appears: tundra up to 1,000 miles wide.
  • The motive: following mammoths and giant bison. Not exploring. Dinner.
  • The official call: Clovis points everywhere, so the Clovis people were first, about 13,000 years ago, through the ice free corridor.

Schematic. Today the crossing is about 85 kilometres of open sea: the Bering Strait.

49–55m • Reveal 2

The Case Cracks

Why the official timeline cannot stand

  • "Eva": a skeleton in a submerged Mexican cave, over 13,600 years old. People were already thousands of miles south before the ice free corridor opened. The Clovis timeline is impossible.
  • The new lead: boats. The Pacific kelp highway: fish, seals, edible sea plants, coastline all the way down.
  • The sunken crime scene: seas rose about 100 metres and drowned the ancient shore. The coastal proof is under deep water. That is why this case is hard, not empty.
55–59m • Reveal 3 • File #10

Two Ways of Knowing

The last piece of evidence changes what kind of case this is

Archaeology asks

When? Which route? It treats arrival as an open puzzle: date it, revise it, argue it. Current call: no single route explains all the evidence. Multiple waves, over thousands of years.

Oral tradition answers

Many Indigenous nations hold that their people have been here since time immemorial: living archives, carried by accurate oral tradition, connecting the people to the origin of the land. Not a puzzle. A memory.

59–60m • Close the File

What This Case Proves

And what it leaves open

"Ancestral and traditional territory" is not a feeling. It is a claim: the longest continuous presence anyone can show, by dig or by story.
