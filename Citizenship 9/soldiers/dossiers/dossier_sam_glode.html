Sam Glode (Gloade)

Sergeant · 64th Battalion, CEF; No. 1 Canadian Tunnelling Company

First World War (1915-1919) Nova Scotia Milton, Queens County, Nova Scotia

A Mi'kmaq lumberjack and hunting guide from Milton, Nova Scotia, who became a tunneller on the Western Front, dug underground at Messines and Vimy Ridge, and earned the Distinguished Conduct Medal for removing 450 demolition charges in front of the advancing army. He is counted among the most decorated First Nations soldiers of the war.

Where This Came From: This dossier is built from the Wikipedia articles on Sam Gloade and Samuel Glode, which cite Thomas Raddall's 1944 interviews with Glode published as 'Sam Glode: Travels of a Micmac' in Cape Breton's Magazine, no. 35 (January 1984), and the Queen's County Advance. It is not a canadianletters.ca collection.

Three-Paragraph Remembrance Report Notes

Target Length: ~300 Words Total (90-110 Words Per Paragraph)
Paragraph 1
Identity & Origins
  • Hometown: Born about 1877 in Nova Scotia and living at Milton, Queens County. A Mi'kmaq man, he worked as a lumberjack and guide. His wife Louisa Francis died in 1905 and he raised his family as a single father.
  • Enlistment: He enlisted in 1915 and trained in England with the 64th Battalion before volunteering for the Canadian Engineers.
  • Skills: Years of hunting and guiding in the woods prepared him for work underground in the dark.
Paragraph 2
Conflict & Strategic Context
  • Conflict & Mandate: First World War. Both sides dug tunnels under no man's land to plant huge mines or to defend against them.
  • Unit: He joined the No. 1 Canadian Tunnelling Company and worked at Messines, Vimy Ridge and Passchendaele, and was at Amiens in 1918.
  • Underground: On one occasion he was in charge of 20 men trapped underground and is credited with digging for hours until he broke through to the surface.
Paragraph 3
Human Cost & Legacy
  • Human Cost: He described the terror of his first shelling at Messines in 1917: green soldiers running up and down the trench as shells burst.
  • Recognition: Promoted to corporal and then sergeant, he won the Distinguished Conduct Medal for removing 450 demolition charges during the advance toward Germany.
  • Legacy: He returned to Milton, died in 1957, and is buried at St. Gregory's cemetery. He is regarded as one of the most highly decorated First Nations soldiers of the war.

June 1917 (interviewed in 1944) Interview Excerpt

From: Sam Glode · To: Thomas Raddall
Sam Glode described his first day under shellfire at the Battle of Messines (1917), in an interview with the author Thomas Raddall: "Late that afternoon the German artillery on Messines Ridge began to shell our trench and kept it up for a long time. They scared us bad, I tell you. We were all green hands, and we would leave our rifles and run along the trench away from shell burst. Then another shell would burst near us and we would run down the trench again. Some fellows got hit and they hollered and there was a lot of blood."
Source Verification: View at Wikipedia: Sam Gloade (quoting Thomas Raddall, Cape Breton's Magazine no. 35)

1916-1918 Service Summary

Archival Transcription
Glode trained with the 64th Battalion, an infantry reinforcement holding unit in England, and then joined the 1st Canadian Tunnelling Company, Canadian Engineers. He dug trenches under Vimy Ridge, patched roadways near Amiens, and defused mines after the war. He worked from La Clytte Camp, close to Ypres in Belgium, for more than a year, and was in the battles of Passchendaele and Amiens (1918). On one occasion he was in charge of 20 soldiers who got trapped underground. He is credited with having dug alone for hours before he was able to burrow a hole to the surface.
Source Verification: View at Wikipedia: Sam Gloade

1918 Distinguished Conduct Medal

Archival Transcription
In 1918 Glode was posted to the 6th Battalion Canadian Engineers and promoted to corporal. He was awarded the Distinguished Conduct Medal for his work removing 450 demolition charges during the advance toward Germany. This made him one of the most highly decorated First Nations combatants of the war. He returned to Nova Scotia after the war and died at Camp Hill Hospital in Halifax in 1957.
Source Verification: View at Wikipedia: Samuel Glode (citing John Boileau, The Canadian Encyclopedia)
