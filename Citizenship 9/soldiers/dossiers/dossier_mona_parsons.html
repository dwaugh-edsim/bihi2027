Mona Parsons

Civilian (Dutch resistance) · Escape-line network sheltering Allied airmen, near Laren, Netherlands

Second World War (1940-1945) Nova Scotia Wolfville, Kings County, Nova Scotia (born in Middleton)

A Nova Scotia actress and nurse who hid Allied airmen in her Dutch home, was sentenced to death by a Nazi tribunal, survived almost four years in German prisons, and in March 1945 escaped on foot and walked into the North Nova Scotia Highlanders. She is the only Canadian woman civilian known to have been imprisoned by the Nazis.

Where This Came From: This dossier is assembled from the Wikipedia article on Mona Parsons (which quotes her own writing and cites Andria Hill's biography), The Canadian Encyclopedia, and her official website. It is not a canadianletters.ca collection.
Sources: Official site: monaparsons.ca · The Canadian Encyclopedia: Mona Parsons · Wikipedia: Mona Parsons
Open Main Source: The Canadian Encyclopedia

Three-Paragraph Remembrance Report Notes

Target Length: ~300 Words Total (90-110 Words Per Paragraph)
Paragraph 1
Identity & Origins
  • Hometown: Born in Middleton, Nova Scotia in 1901 and educated in Wolfville at the Acadia Ladies' Seminary and Acadia University.
  • Early Career: Moved to New York in 1929 as a Ziegfeld Follies chorus girl, then trained as a nurse and graduated in 1935.
  • Marriage: In 1937 she married wealthy Dutch businessman Willem Leonhardt and settled near Laren in the Netherlands.
Paragraph 2
Conflict & Strategic Context
  • Conflict & Mandate: Second World War. Germany invaded the Netherlands in May 1940 and occupied it.
  • Resistance Work: Parsons joined an informal resistance network and sheltered downed Allied airmen at her home, Ingleside. Airmen were passed on to Leiden, where boats took them out to meet British submarines.
  • Arrest and Trial: The network was infiltrated. The Gestapo arrested her on September 29, 1941. At her trial on December 22, 1941 she was found guilty of treason and sentenced to death by firing squad.
Paragraph 3
Human Cost & Legacy
  • Human Cost: Her sentence was commuted to life with hard labour. She was held in German prisons, often ill and hungry, and wrote of 'practically 4 years of isolation'.
  • Escape: On March 24, 1945, while Allied planes bombed the camp, she escaped with Baroness Wendelien van Boetzelaer. They walked for three weeks and Parsons posed as the Baroness's mentally challenged aunt so that no one would hear her Canadian accent.
  • Legacy: Commended by Air Chief Marshal Tedder and General Eisenhower. A statue was unveiled in Wolfville in May 2017 and Canada Post issued a Mona Parsons stamp in November 2023.

Written after the war Memoir Excerpt

From: Mona Parsons
The first year I was ill a lot, weighed only about 94 pounds & was green - night sweats, coughing & diarrhoea every day for 3½ months & often vomiting. Tears have run down my cheeks for hunger. When the diarrhoea got better I was given a pint of soup extra - made from turnip & potato peelings - every day for 6 months & my vitamin tablets which I had been allowed to keep with me. There were no medicines to be had. We slept four in a tiny cell built for one. In all the years of imprisonment I slept always on a straw sack on the floor. I was in solitary once for two weeks, for writing a letter in English. Fortunately no one could read English, otherwise another prisoner might have been involved. I got out of it by saying it was only a little story I was writing to amuse myself. We were not allowed to have pencil or paper. Practically 4 years of isolation.

December 22nd 1941 Trial Account

Archival Transcription
Parsons was tried by a Nazi military tribunal in Amsterdam on December 22, 1941, found guilty of treason, and sentenced to death by firing squad. She is reported to have answered the sentence with such dignified calm that the chief judge allowed her to appeal. The sentence was then commuted to life with hard labour. The words most often reported from that day are her farewell to the judges: "Guten morgen, meine herren" (good morning, gentlemen).
Source Verification: View at Wikipedia: Mona Parsons (and The Canadian Encyclopedia)

March 24th 1945 Escape Account

Archival Transcription
On March 24, 1945, as Allied forces bombed the prison camp, Parsons escaped with young Dutch Baroness Wendelien van Boetzelaer. Although Parsons spoke fluent German, she did not risk speaking it with a Canadian accent. Using her acting skills she posed as the young woman's mentally challenged aunt who could not speak because of a cleft palate. The pair walked and evaded capture for three weeks, trading labour for food and a place to sleep, often in a barn, and covered about 125 km through Germany. The two were separated at Rhede, near the Dutch border. Parsons carried on her act alone, crossed into the Netherlands at Vlagtwedde, told a Dutch farmer she was Canadian and needed to find British troops, and was taken to the North Nova Scotia Highlanders.
Source Verification: View at Wikipedia: Mona Parsons

1945-2023 Honours and Remembrance

Archival Transcription
After the war Parsons received commendations for helping Allied airmen from Air Chief Marshal Lord Arthur Tedder (for the British people) and General Dwight Eisenhower (for the American people). She returned to Nova Scotia in December 1957, later married Major General Harry Foster, and moved back to Wolfville in 1970, where she died in 1976. Historica Canada released a Heritage Minute about her in 2005, a statue titled "The joy is almost too much to bear" was unveiled in Wolfville in May 2017, and Canada Post featured her on a stamp in November 2023.
