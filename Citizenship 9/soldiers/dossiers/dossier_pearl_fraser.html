Margaret Marjory (Pearl) Fraser

Matron (Captain) · Acting Matron, HMHS Llandovery Castle

First World War (1914-1918) Nova Scotia New Glasgow, Pictou County, Nova Scotia

The daughter of a Nova Scotia lieutenant governor, Pearl Fraser trained as a nurse, joined the Canadian army in 1914, and served four years on hospital ships. She was the matron aboard HMHS Llandovery Castle when a German U-boat sank it and then ran down the lifeboats. A surviving sergeant recorded her last words.

Three-Paragraph Remembrance Report Notes

Target Length: ~300 Words Total (90-110 Words Per Paragraph)
Paragraph 1
Identity & Origins
  • Hometown: Born March 20, 1885 in New Glasgow, Nova Scotia, one of seven children of Duncan Cameron Fraser, who was lieutenant governor of Nova Scotia from 1906 to 1910.
  • Training: She trained as a nurse at the Lord Stanley Institute in Ottawa.
  • Enlistment: She enlisted in the Canadian Expeditionary Force in Quebec on September 28, 1914.
Paragraph 2
Conflict & Strategic Context
  • Conflict & Mandate: First World War. Hospital ships carried the wounded from France back to Britain and Canada, and international law protected them from attack.
  • Service: She served on hospital ships in European waters as an acting matron for four years.
  • The Ship: HMHS Llandovery Castle was a Canadian hospital ship with 622 beds. On June 27, 1918 it was torpedoed by the German submarine U-86.
Paragraph 3
Human Cost & Legacy
  • Human Cost: The U-boat captain tried to destroy the evidence by running down the lifeboats and machine-gunning survivors. Only 25 people are known to have survived, and 24 of them were rescued from a single lifeboat.
  • Her Last Words: Sergeant Arthur Knight, in the lifeboat with the nurses, reported that Fraser asked him, 'Sergeant, do you think there is any hope for us?'
  • Legacy: She is commemorated at the Halifax Memorial and on the piper monument in New Glasgow. The sinking was prosecuted at the Leipzig war crimes trials after the war.

June 27th 1918 Eyewitness Report

From: Sgt. Arthur Knight
Sergeant Arthur Knight was on board lifeboat #5 with the nurses. He reported: "Our boat was quickly loaded and lowered to the surface of the water. Then the crew of eight men and myself faced the difficulty of getting free from the ropes holding us to the ship's side. I broke two axes trying to cut ourselves away, but was unsuccessful. With the forward motion and choppy sea the boat all the time was pounding against the ship's side. To save the boat we tried to keep ourselves away by using the oars, and soon every one of the latter were broken. Finally the ropes became loose at the top and we commenced to drift away. We were carried towards the stern of the ship, when suddenly the poop-deck seemed to break away and sink. The suction drew us quickly into the vacuum, the boat tipped over sideways, and every occupant went under. I estimate we were together in the boat about eight minutes. In that whole time I did not hear a complaint or murmur from one of the sisters. There was not a cry for help or any outward evidence of fear. In the entire time I overheard only one remark when the matron, Nursing Sister M.M. Fraser, turned to me as we drifted helplessly towards the stern of the ship and asked: 'Sergeant, do you think there is any hope for us?' I replied, 'No,' seeing myself our helplessness without oars and the sinking condition of the stern of the ship. A few seconds later we were drawn into the whirlpool of the submerged afterdeck, and the last I saw of the nursing sisters was as they were thrown over the side of the boat. All were wearing lifebelts, and of the fourteen two were in their nightdress, the others in uniform. It was doubtful if any of them came to the surface again, although I myself sank and came up three times, finally clinging to a piece of wreckage and being eventually picked up by the captain's boat."

1885-1918 Biographical Source Note

Archival Transcription
Pearl Fraser was born on 20 March 1885 in New Glasgow, Nova Scotia, to Duncan Cameron Fraser and Bessie Grant Fraser. Her father was lieutenant governor of Nova Scotia from 1906 until 1910, and her brother Alistair Fraser served in the same office from 1952 until 1958. She trained as a nurse at the Lord Stanley Institute in Ottawa and enlisted in the Canadian Expeditionary Force in Quebec on 28 September 1914. She served on hospital ships in European waters as an acting matron for four years, until she died aboard HMHS Llandovery Castle on 27 June 1918, aged 33 and single. She received the 1914-15 Star, the British War Medal and the Victory Medal.

June 27th 1918 Sinking of the Hospital Ship

Archival Transcription
HMHS Llandovery Castle was commissioned as a hospital ship on 26 July 1916 and assigned to the Canadian forces, with 622 beds and a medical staff of 102. Firing at a hospital ship was against international law. The captain of the German submarine U-86, Helmut Brümmer-Patzig, wanted to destroy the evidence. When the crew, including the nurses, took to the lifeboats, U-86 surfaced, ran down all but one of the lifeboats and machine-gunned many of the survivors. The destroyer HMS Lysander rescued 24 people in one lifeboat 36 hours later. Among those lost were fourteen nursing sisters from Canada. The incident became infamous internationally and was one of six alleged German war crimes prosecuted at the Leipzig war crimes trials.
