Ethelbert (Curley) Christian

Private · 108th Battalion, then 78th Battalion (Winnipeg Grenadiers), CEF

First World War (1915-1918) Other Canada (Manitoba / Ontario) Enlisted at Selkirk, Manitoba; lived in Toronto, Ontario after the war

A Black veteran who was buried by a shell at Vimy Ridge and lost both arms and both legs, yet became the only known person to survive a quadruple amputation in the First World War. He came home on the hospital ship Llandovery Castle, helped create the attendance allowance for severely disabled veterans, and returned to Vimy in 1936.

Where This Came From: This dossier is built from The Canadian Encyclopedia article by Thomas Toliver Smith (which reproduces items from the Curley Christian Scrapbook held by the Military Museums in Calgary) and the Wikipedia article. Christian was not a Nova Scotian: he enlisted in Manitoba and lived in Toronto. He is included as a national file and because the ship that carried him home links to the Nova Scotia nurses on the Llandovery Castle.
Sources: The Canadian Encyclopedia: Curley Christian · Wikipedia: Curley Christian · Wikipedia: HMHS Llandovery Castle

Three-Paragraph Remembrance Report Notes

Target Length: ~300 Words Total (90-110 Words Per Paragraph)
Paragraph 1
Identity & Origins
  • Hometown: Born most likely April 15, 1882 in Homestead, Pennsylvania (family tradition says 1884, Virginia). He travelled widely and worked as a chain-maker, cook, bricklayer and on a tramp steamer.
  • Enlistment: He crossed from Alaska into Canada and enlisted in the autumn of 1915 in Selkirk, Manitoba, joining the 108th Battalion and later the 78th Battalion (Winnipeg Grenadiers).
  • Records: Much of his biography is contradictory. The Canadian Encyclopedia suggests Black families sometimes altered records to avoid government attention in a racist era.
Paragraph 2
Conflict & Strategic Context
  • Conflict & Mandate: First World War. In April 1917 the Canadian Corps took Vimy Ridge.
  • His Job: Christian delivered supplies to the front trenches, a dangerous task because German guns on the ridge could see for kilometres.
  • The Injury: A shell buried him in a trench; he was found two days later. Gangrene set in, and doctors amputated both lower legs and both forearms.
Paragraph 3
Human Cost & Legacy
  • Human Cost: He became the first and only person known to survive a quadruple amputation in the First World War.
  • The Voyage Home: In September 1917 he sailed to Canada on HMHS Llandovery Castle. Nine months later that hospital ship was torpedoed and sunk with the loss of 234 doctors, nurses and patients.
  • Legacy: He designed a prosthetic attachment for writing letters to other amputees, helped win the attendance allowance for severely disabled veterans, and met King Edward VIII at the 1936 Vimy Pilgrimage.

Primary Source Transcriptions (3 Curated Items)

April 1917 Biographical Source Note

Archival Transcription
Military records give very little detail about Christian's injury, noting only that he was wounded by an artillery shell and that all four limbs had to be amputated because of gangrene. Family stories add more: that he was buried in a trench by high-explosive shelling after a supply drop, and was found two days later barely alive. Stretcher-bearers who first tried to carry him out were killed by enemy artillery; a second team reached him and took him to triage. Both lower legs were removed about five inches below the knee, his left arm about five inches below the elbow, and his right arm about four inches below the elbow. A quadruple amputation at this time was considered highly risky, and some doctors believed he would not live.
Source Verification: View at The Canadian Encyclopedia (Thomas Toliver Smith, 2014)

1917-1920 Biographical Source Note

Archival Transcription
Christian was cared for in Europe until September 1917, when he returned to Toronto aboard the hospital ship Llandovery Castle to be fitted for prosthetics. In 1918 he was transferred to Euclid Hall on Jarvis Street, where he met Cleopatra (Cleo) McPherson, a volunteer aide born in Jamaica. They married on 11 December 1920 and had one son, Douglas. Because Christian needed lifelong care, Cleo and the hospital director worked out a plan for her to care for him at home. The hospital director appealed to the government, which agreed to pay an allowance to full-time caregivers of wounded veterans. That program, now called the attendance allowance, is still offered today.
Source Verification: View at The Canadian Encyclopedia (Thomas Toliver Smith, 2014)

July-August 1936 Biographical Source Note

Archival Transcription
In July 1936 Christian and 6,200 other veterans, many of them blinded by chlorine gas, accepted an invitation to the dedication of the Canadian National Vimy Memorial in France. He travelled aboard the S.S. Duchess of Richmond. Items from his trip, including the guidebook, a concert programme dated 22 August 1936 and newspaper clippings, are preserved in the Curley Christian Scrapbook, courtesy of Sharon Williams and the Military Museums of Calgary. During the trip Christian and a group of blinded Canadian veterans met King Edward VIII. Some newspapers said Christian had approached the king without permission, and he answered the criticism indignantly. According to family accounts he also designed a prosthetic attachment for writing so that he could send letters of encouragement to other military amputees. He died in Toronto on March 15, 1954.
Source Verification: View at The Canadian Encyclopedia (Thomas Toliver Smith, 2014)
