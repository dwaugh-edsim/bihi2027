Private Jeremiah Henry ("Jerry") Jones (East Mountain & Truro, Nova Scotia)Private Jeremiah ("Jerry") Jones served in the First World War with the 106th Battalion (Nova Scotia Rifles) and the Royal Canadian Regiment (RCR) under Regimental #716221. His archival holdings include his fully digitized CEF personnel file at Library and Archives Canada (LAC ID 339139, Service File B4944-S007, digitized at http://data2.collectionscanada.gc.ca/cef/4001-5000/4944-07.pdf), the Nominal Roll of the 106th Battalion (e011092698), a wartime uniform photograph (DND/LAC/PA-001380), and August and September 1917 newspaper articles from the Truro Daily News preserved in the Colchester Historical Society Archives.Born in East Mountain near Truro, Nova Scotia, Jones worked as a teamster (animal-drawn vehicle driver) and lived in Truro with his wife, Ethel Jones. There is a notable historical discrepancy regarding his age that makes a great primary-source detective point for Grade 9 students: while The Canadian Encyclopedia, Legion Magazine, and Wikipedia list his birth date as March 30, 1858—which would make him 58 years old when he enlisted on June 19, 1916, in Truro (13 years above the age limit)—the Canadian Great War Project notes that his attestation paper and 1911 census entry (Jerry Jones) list his birth date as March 29, 1877, and Legion Magazine notes he stated his age as 38 to get in. By the end of 1915, at least 200 Black Canadian volunteers had been rejected by recruiting officers, and Truro's 106th Battalion eventually took on 18 Black recruits. Once overseas, Jones was reassigned to a reluctant Royal Canadian Regiment, where he proved his mettle during the Battle of Vimy Ridge in April 1917. When Canadian troops were pinned down by a German machine-gun nest, Jones volunteered to attack the emplacement alone: "I threw a hand bomb right into the nest and killed about seven of them," Jones recalled years later. "I was going to throw another bomb when they threw up their hands and called for mercy.". He forced the surviving German prisoners to carry their machine gun back and deposit it at the feet of his commanding officer, asking: "Is this thing any good?".Wounded at Vimy Ridge and evacuated to No. 13 Stationary Hospital, Jones was formally discharged in Halifax in early 1918 as medically unfit.(Note: although some publications state he was also wounded at Passchendaele, archival notes on his service file point out he did not participate in Passchendaele.) He returned to Ethel and their large family in Truro, working as a woodsman, farmer, and handyman until his death on November 23, 1950. On September 21, 1917, his sister Martha told the Truro Daily News that Jones "won the DCM by capturing a German machine gun and crew", and on his 79th birthday the newspaper again reported that "His valor won for him a recommendation for the DCM". However, no medal was awarded in his lifetime. After decades of campaigning led by Senator Calvin Ruck and the Jones family, the Canadian government posthumously awarded him the Canadian Forces Medallion for Distinguished Service on February 22, 2010.2. Mona Louise Parsons (Wolfville, Nova Scotia)Mona Louise Parsons (1901–1976) of Wolfville, Nova Scotia, provides your collection with a dramatic Second World War European resistance narrative backed by vivid first-person accounts of her Nazi military trial, imprisonment, and escape. Her primary documentation includes her verbatim recollections of her December 22, 1941 Nazi tribunal and three-year captivity, official postwar commendations from the British Air Marshal and U.S. General (later President) Dwight D. Eisenhower, and her bronze memorial statue unveiled in Wolfville in May 2017.Raised in Wolfville, Parsons performed as a young woman in New York's Ziegfeld Follies before marrying Dutch millionaire Willem in Holland in 1937. When Nazi Germany invaded Holland in May 1940, she joined the Allied underground resistance to shelter Allied airmen and help them escape occupation. Arrested by the Nazis and offered freedom if she betrayed her network, she refused to give up any information and was brought to trial on December 22, 1941. Describing the Nazi prosecutor, she later wrote: "He was a tall, repulsive man with cold, pale blue eyes, stringy colourless hair and heavy thick glasses. He could have had a monocle. He seemed so full of hate, I doubt if he was even pleased with himself... My answers and my determination not to display any emotion plainly infuriated him.". Sentenced to death, she refused to break down: "I knew all eyes were on me, expecting me to burst into tears. I was determined not to humble myself before any of them. As I left the courtroom, I put my heels together and bowed toward the judge, the prosecutor and my German counsel, who were standing together in a group. 'Guten morgen, meine herren,' I said. My action took them by surprise. They stared at me with their mouths open. After they had stopped gaping, they returned my greeting. The judge followed me from the courtroom and approached with outstretched hand.".Her sentence was commuted to prison labour in Germany, where she endured severe malnutrition for over three years: "The first year I was ill a lot, weighed only 94 pounds and was green. Night sweats, coughing and diarrhea every day for three-and-a-half months and often vomiting. Tears have run down my cheeks for hunger… There were no medicines to be had. We slept four in a tiny cell built for one. In all the years of imprisonment, I slept always on a straw sack on the floor.". Losing hair and teeth from starvation, she intentionally performed her prison work poorly as an act of quiet resistance until escaping in March 1945 alongside Dutch Baroness Wendelien ("Wendy"). Walking across Germany for three weeks until their shoes wore out and they had to go barefoot, Parsons worried her Canadian accent would expose them if she spoke German, so she used her acting skills to pose as Wendy's mute, mentally-challenged aunt with a cleft palate. "There was nothing funny about our situation," Parsons recalled. "We were far behind the Nazi lines and at any moment might be recaptured. If that happened, our lives wouldn't be worth a plugged pfennig. Wendy said, 'if they catch us this time, we will be shot.'". After surviving a close call where they accidentally hailed an SS policeman and spent the night at his house, they crossed into Holland, where Parsons reached Canadian lines and found herself face-to-face with the North Nova Scotia Highlanders—and Captain Robbins Elliot, whose father back in Wolfville had been her own family physician.3. Captain Nichola Kathleen Sarah Goddard, MSM (Antigonish, Nova Scotia & Charlottetown, Prince Edward Island)Captain Nichola Goddard (1980–2006) brings your collection into the 21st century as the first female Canadian soldier killed in a combat role during the War in Afghanistan. Born May 2, 1980, in Madang, Papua New Guinea, to educators Tim and Sally Goddard, she lived in seven communities across Canada by age 18—including Black Lake, Saskatchewan, and Edmonton, Alberta—before moving to Antigonish, Nova Scotia, where she attended high school. Her parents later made their home in Charlottetown, Prince Edward Island, where Captain Goddard is prominently commemorated alongside her memorials in Antigonish and Calgary.Serving in the Royal Canadian Horse Artillery (based in Shilo, Manitoba) and deployed to Afghanistan with the Princess Patricia's Canadian Light Infantry (PPCLI) as a Forward Observation Officer, Goddard directed artillery fire from a Light Armoured Vehicle (LAV). When a commander ordered tarps hung in the sleeping quarters a month after her arrival to separate female soldiers from males, she spoke out against the division. At the same time, her letters home to her parents and husband Jason reflected deep empathy for the people of Afghanistan. Believing that the military's task was to create a stable environment where civilian development work could flourish, she once told her father, "I do what I do so you can do what you do". In a battlefield letter home shared by her mother Sally, Nichola wrote: "It seems to me that we have such a burden of responsibility, to make the world a better place for those who were born into far worse circumstances… It is very humbling to be here – part of something so much bigger than myself. Love as always, Nichola.".On May 17, 2006, during a firefight with Taliban insurgents, an explosion struck her LAV and shrapnel hit the 26-year-old captain in the back of the head, killing her instantly. She was the 16th Canadian soldier—and at that point the highest-ranking Canadian soldier and first Canadian woman—killed in combat in Afghanistan. Her parents established the Nichola Goddard Foundation to support women in the military and female veterans; as her mother Sally stated from Charlottetown, "Her legacy piece has meant that her death has not been without purpose. I hope Nichola would be proud of what we've done.".4. Private Ethelbert ("Curley") Christian (78th Battalion — Vimy Ridge & Llandovery Castle Survivor)Private Ethelbert ("Curley") Christian (c. 1882/1884–1954) is featured on Slide 2 of your intro deck ("enlisted NS · survived Vimy, rebuilt"). Important archival note for your slide deck: military records show Christian was likely born April 15, 1882 (or 1884) in Homestead, Pennsylvania, worked as a chain-maker, cook, bricklayer, and aboard a tramp steamer to Alaska, and actually enlisted in Selkirk, Manitoba, in 1915 before transferring to the 78th Battalion (Winnipeg Grenadiers). However, his story links directly to your Nova Scotia curriculum in two ways. First, he fought at the Battle of Vimy Ridge (April 1917) in the 78th Battalion—the exact same unit as your current Pictou dossier subject, Pte. George Roderick Chisholm. Second, after suffering catastrophic injuries at Vimy Ridge that required the amputation of all four limbs—making him Canada's only known First World War quadruple amputee—he was carried back to Canada aboard the hospital ship HMHS Llandovery Castle (which nine months later was torpedoed and sunk by a German U-boat with the loss of 234 doctors, nurses, and patients, including Matron Fraser of Nova Scotia's No. 2 Stationary Hospital); Veterans Affairs Canada also notes his home-front connections in Nova Scotia.Christian's primary-source archive—the Curley Christian Scrapbook, preserved by Sharon Williams and the Military Museums of Calgary, alongside his digitized attestation paper—documents how he rebuilt his life after losing both arms and both legs. A creative thinker, he designed custom equipment to carry out everyday tasks, including a prosthetic attachment for writing, which enabled him to write letters to other military amputees and veterans offering guidance and encouragement. He advocated tirelessly for disabled veterans, married after a hospital romance, and in August 1936 sailed aboard the S.S. Duchess of Richmond on the Vimy Pilgrimage to France, where he met King Edward VIII and helped introduce a group of blinded Canadian veterans to the King.5. Sergeant Sam Glode (Gloade) (Queens County & Cape Breton, Nova Scotia)Sergeant Sam Glode (also spelled Gloade) was a Mi'kmaq lumberjack and hunting guide from Queens County, Nova Scotia, who enlisted in Nova Scotia's 64th Battalion, CEF (the same battalion in which your Stewiacke Rhodes Scholar dossier subject, Lt. Harry Austin McCleave, enlisted) before transferring in 1916 to the No. 1 Canadian Tunnelling Company. His story is preserved in a remarkable first-person oral history interview published in a 1983 issue of Cape Breton's Magazine and profiled in Legion Magazine.Explaining why he and fellow Mi'kmaq guide John Francis signed up, Glode recalled: "We knew about the war overseas. And we knew the Canadian army was paying $1.10 a day, besides your clothes and grub. So, John said to me, 'Sam, let's go to the war. It can't be no worse than this.'". Assigned to the No. 1 Canadian Tunnelling Company on the Western Front, Glode dug deep underground tunnels beneath the German trenches to pack them with explosives, where German counter-tunnelling nearly killed him. When an underground blast collapsed his tunnel, Glode was trapped in pitch darkness with the air pipe severed: "All of a sudden everything went black. I felt like somebody had hit me on the head with a big club…. When I came to my senses… my ears were singing like a steam whistle was inside them…. I noticed the air was very bad and when I tried to light a candle, it wouldn't light.". Relying on his Mi'kmaq woodsman's sense of terrain, he remembered the contour of the surface above him and dug upward with a shovel: "It's something to be an Indian, after all. You look at a piece of country and it's like a picture in your mind. I remembered the long dip of the ground between our front-line trench and the Germans. I keep picking away, the air got worse and worse…. At last, I struck with a shovel and went through turf and there was daylight and a rush of cold air.". Though he had broken through into No Man's Land, the fresh air kept the trapped tunnellers alive until a rescue party tapped on the collapsed air pipe and dug them out.After only a few days' rest, Glode and his crew went right back underground: "The whole job took about a year," said Glode. "We then worked for 20 days carrying explosives in metal boxes that must have weighed about 50 pounds [23 kilograms] each. We knew the tunnels were to be blown. When the time came, we were all watching from the top of a little hill. The ground shook to and fro like it was shivering. Then we saw flames shoot up high in the dark over the ridge. Then the guns opened up, hundreds of guns. That was some noise, I tell you. Along towards daylight the infantry went over no man's land and up the ridge.". Later in the war, Glode also removed more than 450 explosive charges, and his work earned him promotion to Sergeant before he returned to Nova Scotia to guide hunting and fishing parties.7. Rev. Capt. William Andrew White & the Men of No. 2 Construction Battalion (Pictou, Truro, & Africville, Nova Scotia)Authorized on July 5, 1916, No. 2 Construction Battalion, CEF, was the first and only all-Black battalion in Canadian military history. Its archival record—recently inscribed on the UNESCO Canada Memory of the World Register in November 2024—is completely digitized and accessible for student projects: Library and Archives Canada holds the personal diary of Chaplain William Andrew White (believed to be the only firsthand personal diary written by a member of the unit), the unit's daily War Diary (available on LAC Co-Lab), and digitized personnel files for every soldier (such as the attestation paper of Arthur Bright), while the Nova Scotia Archives hosts the searchable Nominal Roll (https://archives.novascotia.ca/2construction/).Before the battalion was formed, Black Canadians volunteering at recruiting stations were rejected in droves after being told "this is a white man's war", "we do not want a checker board army", or that they "lacked the valour, discipline, and intelligence to fight a modern war". Following protests and letters from Black Canadian leaders and communities, No. 2 Construction Battalion was mobilized in Pictou, Nova Scotia, moved to Truro, Nova Scotia, in September 1916, and trained under Lieut.-Col. Daniel Hugh Sutherland, a Nova Scotia-born railway contractor. Of the unit's 605 men, nearly half came from Nova Scotia. Its chaplain was Reverend Captain William Andrew White, BA, DD (born 1874), a Baptist minister who held the distinction of being the only Black commissioned officer in the British Army ("The Officers, [of the No. 2 CB] with the exception of the Chaplain, who has the distinction of being the only Coloured Officer in the British Army, are all White", noted the unit's War Diary). When White's grandnephew Anthony Sherwood found White's 100-year-old diary in 1998, he noted: "He bore his soul. He wrote everything that he was feeling: the pain, the hurt.".Embarking from Halifax on March 28, 1917, aboard S.S. Southland (and reconstituted in England as No. 2 Construction Company), the men deployed to France in May 1917 attached to the Canadian Forestry Corps near Supt (with a detachment of 180 men accompanying Rev. White to Alençon in December 1917). As recorded in the War Diary, their workday began at "5.00am Reveille. 5.30am Breakfast. 6.40am Parade forms up and working parties move off in sufficient time to reach various places of employment at 7.00am.". Cutting trees, operating sawmills, building logging railways, maintaining water systems, and loading Canadian Corps tramways with ammunition, they supplied the lumber essential for Allied frontline trenches. Students can profile either Rev. White or individual Nova Scotia soldiers from the unit, such as William Lawrence Paris (Colchester County, NS), Private Charlie Some (born in Natal, South Africa, who lived in Africville, Halifax, before the war and was murdered in France in September 1918 and buried in Supt Churchyard), or Private Victor Carvery (from Africville, Nova Scotia, who died on November 20, 1918, and is buried in Delsaux Farm Cemetery, Beugny). After returning to Halifax in mid-January 1919, some veterans in Truro had their houses stoned, and their service faded from public memory until Nova Scotia activist and Senator Calvin Ruck (inspired by seeing older Black railway porters wearing the No. 2 lapel pin) and Capt. (retd.) George Borden's poem "Black Soldiers' Lament" helped lead to the Government of Canada's National Apology in Truro on July 9, 2022.8. Lance Corporal John Shiwak (Sikoak) (Rigolet, Labrador)Born in 1889 in the Inuit community of Rigolet, Labrador, Lance Corporal John Shiwak (Sikoak) of the Royal Newfoundland Regiment (Regimental #1735) gives your students a poignant Indigenous perspective from Labrador during the First World War. His archival records at The Rooms Provincial Archives of Newfoundland and Labrador include his uniform portrait (Item E 29-45), a pre-war group photo with the Legion of Frontiersmen (Item IGA 10-25), and his digitized service file, complemented by his verbatim wartime letters and the memoirs of his comrades (including Captain Sydney Frost's A Blue Puttee at War and fellow soldier Howard Morry).An experienced Inuk hunter and trapper in Rigolet, Shiwak enlisted in the First (later Royal) Newfoundland Regiment on July 24, 1915. He was still in training when the regiment was devastated at Beaumont-Hamel on the Somme, and subsequently joined the ranks on the Western Front, where he was recognized among "The Men of the Royal Newfoundland Regiment who Saved Monchy" in April 1917 and gained renown as an exceptional scout and sharpshooter. Yet the industrialized killing and severe isolation from Labrador took a heavy emotional toll on him. Comrade Howard Morry recalled: "He was shy and lonely. Often he'd say 'Will it ever be over?' He sure was a great shot and had a lot of notches on his rifle. He said sniping was like swatching seals"—scanning open water or ice for surfacing seals.As the war dragged into the autumn of 1917, Shiwak had no close friends at the front to keep warm his connection to Labrador. In a September 1917 letter, he lamented: "I have no letters from home since July. There will be no more now till the ice breaks.". As his correspondent later wrote: "And in his last he longed again for the old hunting days. Labrador, that had never satisfied his ambitions, looked warm and friendly to him now… That was in mid-November. A month later an official envelope came to me. Inside was my last letter. On its face was the soulless stamp. 'Deceased'.". Killed in action in France in mid-November 1917 alongside six other soldiers, Shiwak's gravesite was never located; his name is permanently etched in bronze on the Beaumont-Hamel Newfoundland Memorial in France and on the war memorial in St. John's.Part 3: Expanding Newfoundland, PEI, and Nursing Sister DossiersTo balance the provincial filter buttons in index.html—where Newfoundland currently has only 2 entries and PEI has 3—and to broaden the experiences of underage recruits and First World War nursing sisters, these six additions provide rich documentation.9. Sergeant Thomas ("Tommy") Ricketts, VC (St. John's, Newfoundland)In September 1916, Thomas ("Tommy") Ricketts of St. John's, Newfoundland, followed in his older brother's footsteps and enlisted in the 1st Battalion, Newfoundland Regiment (later the Royal Newfoundland Regiment, Regimental #3102). He was only 15 years old, but lied about his age and told recruiting officials he was the required 18 years and 3 months. Departing Newfoundland in January 1917 and training in Scotland before deploying to France in June 1917 at age 16, Ricketts fought on the Western Front through 1918.During the Hundred Days Offensive on October 14, 1918, Private Ricketts saved his entire platoon when his Lewis machine gun ran out of ammunition under heavy enemy fire. His fearless actions allowed British forces to advance and capture "four field guns, four machine guns and eight prisoners", which "undoubtedly saved many lives". Promoted to Sergeant and awarded both the French Croix de Guerre avec Etoile d'Or (War Cross with Gold Star) and the Victoria Cross, Ricketts—at 17½ years old—became the youngest soldier in the British Commonwealth to earn the Victoria Cross during the First World War.For Grade 9 students examining "what the war cost them and how they lived after," Ricketts's postwar life in St. John's is especially memorable. Disliking talks about the war and trying to avoid the spotlight—even refusing to come out of his shop when Princess Elizabeth parked outside his drugstore during a visit to St. John's until he reluctantly emerged for a brief greeting—he worked quietly for decades as a downtown St. John's pharmacist. Caring gently for colicky babies, children with cuts and bruises, and elderly residents with arthritic joints, he was beloved in the community; one customer whose wounds Ricketts had just dressed looked at him and said, "My God, Tommy, from beggars to kings, you serves [sic] us all.". Ricketts died of a heart attack in his St. John's drugstore in 1967 and is buried in the Anglican Cemetery on Forest Road, where his tombstone bears the Church Lads' Brigade motto: "Fight the Good Fight.". His uniform portraits and pharmacy photographs are preserved at The Rooms Archives and The Canadian Encyclopedia.10.
