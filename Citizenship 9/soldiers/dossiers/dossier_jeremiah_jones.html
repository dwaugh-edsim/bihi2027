Jeremiah (Jerry) Jones

Private · 106th Battalion (Nova Scotia Rifles), then Royal Canadian Regiment (#716221)

First World War (1916-1918) Nova Scotia East Mountain and Truro, Colchester County, Nova Scotia

A Black Nova Scotian teamster from Truro who enlisted in 1916 although he was well over the age limit, then captured a German machine gun and its crew at Vimy Ridge. He was recommended for the Distinguished Conduct Medal but never received it; the Canadian government honoured him in 2010, sixty years after his death.

Where This Came From: This dossier is assembled from his digitized service file at Library and Archives Canada, the Truro Daily News reports of 1917 (as cited by Wikipedia), and The Canadian Encyclopedia. It is not a canadianletters.ca collection.

Three-Paragraph Remembrance Report Notes

Target Length: ~300 Words Total (90-110 Words Per Paragraph)
Paragraph 1
Identity & Origins
  • Hometown: Born in East Mountain near Truro, Nova Scotia. Worked as a teamster (a driver of horse-drawn wagons) and lived in Truro with his wife Ethel; they raised nine children.
  • Enlistment: Enlisted in Truro in June 1916 with the 106th Battalion (Nova Scotia Rifles), at a time when recruiters were turning away Black volunteers.
  • Age Puzzle: His birth year is given as 1858 or 1877, and he told recruiters he was 38. Sources still disagree about which date is right.
Paragraph 2
Conflict & Strategic Context
  • Conflict & Mandate: First World War (1914-1918). At Vimy Ridge in April 1917 all four Canadian divisions attacked together for the first time.
  • Unit: Once overseas he was moved into the Royal Canadian Regiment, a regular army regiment.
  • The Action: Newspaper reports from 1917 say he captured a German machine gun, forced its crew to carry it back, and set it down at the feet of his commanding officer.
Paragraph 3
Human Cost & Legacy
  • Human Cost: Wounded at Vimy Ridge and discharged in Halifax in early 1918 as medically unfit. He died on November 23, 1950.
  • Recognition Denied: The Truro Daily News reported that he was recommended for the Distinguished Conduct Medal, but no medal was awarded in his lifetime.
  • Legacy: A new gravestone was placed with full military honours in 2000. On February 22, 2010 the Canadian government awarded him the Canadian Forces Medallion for Distinguished Service. His grandson was the Nova Scotia activist Rocky Jones.

July 29th 1917 (published August 17th 1917) Newspaper Account

From: A Truro soldier at Witley Camp · To: Truro Daily News
A letter from a Truro soldier at Witley Camp, Surrey, England, written July 29, 1917 and printed in the Truro Daily News, said that Jones: "had captured a German machine gun, forced the crew to carry it back to our lines, and, depositing it at the feet of the CO. said;- 'Is this thing any good?'"
Source Verification: View at Wikipedia: Jeremiah Jones (quoting the Truro Daily News)

September 21st 1917 Newspaper Account

From: Martha (his sister) and the Truro Daily News
On September 21, 1917 Jones's sister Martha told the Truro Daily News that Jones "won the DCM by capturing a German machine gun and crew". On his 79th birthday the newspaper again alluded to the medal, reporting: "His valor won for him a recommendation for the DCM". No records have surfaced to show that he ever actually received the Distinguished Conduct Medal.
Source Verification: View at Wikipedia: Jeremiah Jones (quoting the Truro Daily News)

1916-1918 Service File

Archival Transcription
Jones's Canadian Expeditionary Force personnel file (regimental number 716221) has been digitized by Library and Archives Canada. The scanned PDF includes his attestation paper, the form a recruit signed to join the army. Compare the birth date written there with the dates given in books and newspapers.
Source Verification: View at Library and Archives Canada (CEF personnel file, PDF)

February 22nd 2010 Official Recognition

Archival Transcription
On February 22, 2010 the Canadian government honoured Jones after his death with the Canadian Forces Medallion for Distinguished Service. Senator Calvin Ruck had lobbied for most of his adult life for a posthumous medal for Jones. Ruck argued that the racist climate of the time kept Jones and other Black soldiers from getting their due, and he felt the medal should honour all Black veterans. Ruck died in 2004 without seeing it.
