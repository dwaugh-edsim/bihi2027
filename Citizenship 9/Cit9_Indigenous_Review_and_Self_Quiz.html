Module 1 • Deep Time & Ancient Migrations

1. The First Peoples: Beringia, Clovis First & The Kelp Highway

Understanding the history of Indigenous presence in the Americas requires looking at geological deep time and the ongoing evolution of archaeological science alongside Indigenous Knowledge.

🌍 Beringia Land Bridge
During the last Ice Age (~13,000–20,000+ years ago), massive continental ice sheets trapped ocean water. Sea levels dropped by ~100 metres, exposing a vast, habitable tundra grassland plain up to 1,600 km (1,000 miles) wide between Siberia and Alaska.
🦣 The Migration Motive
Ancient Siberian hunter-gatherers were not "exploring"; they were mobile family bands following the seasonal migration of Ice Age megafauna—specifically woolly mammoths, steppe bison, and caribou—which provided vital meat, hides, sinew, and bone tools.
🏹 Clovis First Theory
The traditional scientific hypothesis taught that humans first entered North America around 13,000–13,500 years ago by walking through a narrow inland "ice-free corridor" between the Laurentide and Cordilleran glaciers using fluted Clovis spear points.
🌊 Kelp Highway Theory
The modern coastal migration model suggests seafaring peoples traveled southward along the Pacific rim by watercraft, subsisting on rich kelp forest ecosystems (fish, shellfish, seals, sea lions, and edible seaweed) long before inland corridors opened.
⚠️ The Shattered Paradigm: Eva of Naharon

In the early 2000s, cave divers discovered the 13,600+ year-old skeletal remains of a woman nicknamed "Eva of Naharon" inside a flooded underground cave system (cenote) in Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula. Because Eva lived over 13,600 years ago in Central America, humans were already flourishing thousands of kilometres south long before the northern Canadian inland ice corridor was passable. This physical evidence disproved the "Clovis First" model.

🌊 Why Coastal Camps Are Drowned Underwater

When the Ice Age ended and the continental glaciers melted, global sea levels rose by 100 metres. The original coastal shorelines and campsites used by early maritime travelers now sit submerged on the Pacific continental shelf under deep ocean water.

Module 2 • Indigenous Territory & Early Treaties

2. Mi'kma'ki & The Peace and Friendship Treaties (1725–1779)

Nova Scotia sits within Mi'kma'ki, the ancestral and unceded territory of the Mi'kmaw people. Understanding the legal nature of the Peace and Friendship Treaties is foundational to Canadian citizenship.

Treaty / Principle Date & Key Figures Core Terms & Constitutional Meaning
Unceded Land Since Time Immemorial Mi'kmaw sovereignty and land title was never signed away, never sold, and never surrendered through war or conquest. Nova Scotia is unceded territory.
Peace & Friendship 1725–1779 (Maritimes) Solemn government-to-government peace agreements and military alliances between the British Crown and the Mi'kmaq, Maliseet, and Passamaquoddy. No land was ever ceded or surrendered (unlike Western Numbered Treaties).
The 1752 Treaty Gov. Peregrine Hopson & Chief Jean-Baptiste Cope Guaranteed First Nations the "free liberty of hunting and fishing as usual" without hindrance or government interference.
The 1760–61 Treaties British Crown & Mi'kmaw Chiefs Included the landmark "Truckhouse Clause": the Crown promised to maintain trading posts where the Mi'kmaq could trade their harvested fish, furs, and game for European necessaries.
Royal Proclamation 1763 (King George III) Recognized Indigenous land rights across British North America; strictly decreed that Indigenous territory could only be acquired by the Crown through formal, public treaties.
"We Are All Treaty People" Constitutional Principle Treaties are reciprocal two-way pacts. Non-Indigenous Canadians have the legal right to settle, build homes, own land, and govern in Nova Scotia directly through the promises made by the Crown in the treaties.
📜 What Did "Necessaries" Mean in 1760?

In 1760, "necessaries" did not mean luxury goods or open-ended commercial profit. It meant the basic essentials of life needed to sustain a family and community: food, warm clothing, shelter materials, powder, shot, and harvesting tools.

Module 3 • Systemic Racism & Wrongful Conviction

3. Donald Marshall Jr. & The 1971 Wrongful Conviction

Donald Marshall Jr. (Junior Marshall) was an iconic Mi'kmaw leader from Membertou First Nation whose life challenged and transformed the Canadian legal system twice: first through his wrongful murder conviction, and later through his defence of treaty rights.

🔪 Wentworth Park (May 28, 1971)
In Sydney, NS, 17-year-old Donald Marshall Jr. and his Black acquaintance Sandy Seale were approached by Roy Ebsary. Ebsary suddenly stabbed Seale in the stomach and slashed Marshall's arm. Seale died of his injuries; Marshall ran to get help.
🔍 Police Tunnel Vision & Bias
Sydney police immediately focused exclusively on Marshall due to racial prejudice and assumptions of Indigenous guilt. Investigators ignored the elderly knife-wielding stranger described by Marshall and failed to search the area.
🛑 Coerced Teenage Witnesses
Police subjected vulnerable teenage witnesses (John Pratico and Maynard Chant) to coercive interrogations, threatening them until they signed fabricated statements claiming they saw Marshall stab Seale.
⛓️ 11 Years Wrongfully Imprisoned
Convicted by an all-white jury in 1971, Marshall served 11 painful years in maximum-security federal penitentiaries (Dorchester and Springhill) for a crime he never committed before being exonerated in 1982/83. The real killer, Roy Ebsary, was convicted of manslaughter.
⚖️ The 1989 Royal Commission (The Hickman Inquiry)

Led by Chief Justice T. Alexander Hickman, the public inquiry determined that systemic racism infected every stage of the Nova Scotia justice system—the Sydney police, Crown prosecutors, defence attorneys, and trial judges. In response, Nova Scotia created the independent Public Prosecution Service (PPS) in 1990, taking charging and prosecution decisions out of the hands of police officers to eliminate bias.

Module 4 • Sentencing Equity & Restorative Justice

4. Gladue Rights & Restorative Justice

The Marshall Inquiry's revelations of systemic over-incarceration of Indigenous people directly sparked national criminal justice reforms.

⚖️ Criminal Code Section 718.2(e)
Enacted in 1996, this federal law directs judges that "all available sanctions, other than imprisonment, that are reasonable in the circumstances, should be considered for all offenders, with particular attention to the circumstances of Aboriginal offenders."
🏛️ R. v. Gladue (1999)
The Supreme Court of Canada affirmed that judges are legally required to evaluate the unique systemic and historical factors affecting Indigenous people at sentencing hearings to combat severe over-incarceration.
📋 The Gladue Report
A comprehensive biographical evaluation prepared for the judge detailing the offender's personal history, residential school trauma, the Sixties Scoop, poverty, racism, and culturally grounded healing plans.
🌱 Restorative Justice Principles
Focuses on repairing relationships, addressing the root causes of harm, and community reintegration rather than retributive punishment (warehousing individuals in prison cells).
Module 5 • The Eel Catch & Constitutional Supremacy

5. Donald Marshall Jr. & The 1993 Eel Catch (R. v. Marshall)

Twenty-two years after his wrongful arrest, Donald Marshall Jr. took another stand for justice that redefined Canadian constitutional law.

🎣 The 1993 Incident at Pomquet Harbour

In August 1993, Donald Marshall Jr. and his partner Jane McMillan caught 463 pounds of adult eels in Pomquet Harbour, Antigonish County, selling them for $787.10 to support themselves. The federal Department of Fisheries and Oceans (DFO) charged Marshall with fishing without a commercial licence, fishing during a closed season, and selling fish without a licence under the Fisheries Act.

📜 Marshall's Legal Defense
Marshall admitted catching and selling the eels, but pled not guilty, arguing that as a Mi'kmaw person, he was exercising his protected treaty rights under the 1760 Peace and Friendship Treaty.
🇨🇦 Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982
"The existing aboriginal and treaty rights of the aboriginal peoples of Canada are hereby recognized and affirmed." Section 35 elevated treaty rights into supreme constitutional law.
⚖️ Hierarchy of Law
In Canada, when an ordinary federal regulation (such as a DFO seasonal closure) conflicts with a Section 35 constitutional treaty right, the treaty right takes precedence unless the government can prove a compelling conservation need.
Module 6 & 7 • The Supreme Court Ruling & Modern Disputes

6. The 1999 Marshall Ruling, "Moderate Livelihood" & Modern Conflicts

On September 17, 1999, the Supreme Court of Canada acquitted Donald Marshall Jr. of all charges in a landmark ruling that altered resource access across Eastern Canada.

🏆 Core Supreme Court Findings in R. v. Marshall (1999)
  • The Truckhouse Clause is an Enduring Covenant: The 1760 promise was not a temporary historical arrangement; it continues to protect the Mi'kmaw right to harvest fish and wildlife to trade commercially.
  • Definition of "Moderate Livelihood": Modernized the concept of "necessaries" to mean food, clothing, housing, and modest economic security. It does NOT grant a right to build industrial corporate fleets or accumulate open-ended wealth.
  • The "Undefined Trap": The Supreme Court never defined an exact dollar figure, trap count, or seasonal timetable for a "moderate livelihood," urging First Nations and Canada to negotiate.
  • Marshall 2 Clarification (Nov 1999): The Court clarified that the federal government (DFO) retains the power to regulate Indigenous fisheries, but ONLY for genuine conservation purposes and following meaningful consultation.
🦞 2020 St. Mary's Bay Lobster Dispute
In Saulnierville, NS, Sipekne'katik First Nation launched their own self-regulated "moderate livelihood" lobster fishery outside DFO seasons under their 1760 treaty rights. Tensions erupted as commercial fishers protested, leading to trap destruction and the burning of a lobster pound. The crisis exposed Canada's 21-year failure to implement a moderate livelihood framework.
🌿 Sovereign Trading Posts / Dispensaries
In the 2020s, Mi'kmaw entrepreneurs opened trading posts on unceded reserve land under the 1760 Truckhouse Clause, clashing with RCMP enforcement under the federal Cannabis Act over self-regulation and treaty trade rights.
🏛️ Civic Responsibility of Grade 9 Citizens
Grade 9 citizens must recognize that treaties are binding constitutional contracts. Understanding treaty harvesting as legal rights rather than "poaching," challenging racist misinformation, and acknowledging unceded land are essential civic duties.
