ACT I • WENTWORTH PARK, SYDNEY • MAY 1971
The Wrongful Conviction of 17-Year-Old Donald Marshall Jr.

Wentworth Park, Sydney, NS (May 28, 1971)

  • Donald Marshall Jr. (17, Membertou First Nation) and Sandy Seale (black youth from Whitney Pier) meet up in the park at night.
  • Encounter with Roy Ebsary (58, white man with a history of carrying dangerous knives).
  • An argument breaks out; Ebsary suddenly pulls an antique knife and stabs Sandy Seale. Marshall is stabbed in the arm trying to defend him.
Sandy Seale dies in the hospital. Donald Marshall Jr. voluntarily goes to the police station to report the attack.

Police Tunnel Vision & Coerced Witnesses

  • Sydney Police immediately decide the 17-year-old Mi'kmaw teen is the killer, ignoring all evidence pointing to Ebsary.
  • Detectives intimidate and coerce teenage witnesses into signing false statements.
  • Marshall is convicted of murder and sentenced to life imprisonment.
  • 11 Years in Maximum Security: Marshall spends over a decade in federal prison for a crime he never committed.
The real killer walked free for over a decade while police protected their conviction.

ACT II • EXONERATION & GLADUE RIGHTS • 1989–1999
The 1989 Royal Commission & Gladue Principles

The 1989 Royal Commission Report

  • Donald Marshall Jr. was finally acquitted and freed in 1983. In 1989, a formal Royal Commission released its report.
  • Explicit finding of systemic racism: Concluded the justice system failed Marshall at every single step “because he was an Indigenous person.”
  • Roy Ebsary was finally tried and convicted of manslaughter.
  • Sparked the creation of the Mi'kmaw Legal Support Network (MLSN) and restorative justice in NS.
Lesson: Racism wasn't just individual prejudice; it was built into the institutional machinery of the police and courts.

Gladue Rights (Supreme Court, 1999)

  • In 1999 (*R. v. Gladue*), the Supreme Court ruled on Criminal Code Section 718.2(e).
  • The Gladue Principle: Canadian judges are legally required to consider the historic trauma, residential schools, and systemic discrimination facing Indigenous people.
  • Courts must actively look for restorative justice and alternatives to prison instead of reflexive jail time.
A direct national reform born from the injustice suffered by Donald Marshall Jr.

ACT III • POMQUET HARBOUR • AUGUST 1993
463 Pounds of Eels: The Federal Ambush

The Catch: Pomquet Harbour, NS

  • August 1993: Ten years after his release from prison, Marshall and his wife go out in a small boat on Pomquet Harbour.
  • They catch 463 pounds of adult eels.
  • Sold the catch to a South Carolina buyer for $787.10.
  • The money was used strictly for family survival: to buy groceries and pay rent.
He wasn't running a commercial corporation; he was fishing to feed his family.

The Federal Ambush (DFO Swoop)

  • Department of Fisheries and Oceans (DFO) officers intercept him, seize his boat and nets.
  • 3 Federal Criminal Charges:
    • Fishing out of commercial season
    • Fishing without a federal license
    • Using an illegal net
Under Canada's Fisheries Act, he is 100% guilty. Open and shut.

ACT IV • THE 1760 DEFENSE • FORT EDWARD
The 233-Year-Old Piece of Parchment
“Saving only unto the said tribe the free liberty of Hunting & Fishing as usual... And that a Truckhouse shall be established at Fort Edward or elsewhere, for furnishing the Indians with necessaries in Exchange for their Peltry and other commodities.”
— Treaty of Peace and Friendship, Signed 1760 at Windsor / Fort Edward

What The King Promised in 1760

  • The Mi'kmaq retain free harvesting “as usual” (no Crown permits or calendar seasons).
  • The British promise a Truckhouse (trading post) to exchange resources for necessaries (food, clothes, shelter).

What Is NOT in the Treaty

  • There is zero land surrendered or sold.
  • Nova Scotia and Mi'kma'ki are unceded territory.
  • This was a nation-to-nation peace pact, not a conquest.

ACT V • 1999 ACQUITTAL & THE FATAL FLAW
The Acquittal — And The Explosion at Burnt Church & St. Mary's Bay

The Supreme Court Ruling (1999)

  • Donald Marshall Jr. is ACQUITTED of all charges.
  • Section 35 of Canada's Constitution (1982) protects historic treaties as supreme law.
  • The Truckhouse clause means Mi'kmaq have an inherent right to catch and trade fish for a “Moderate Livelihood” (food, shelter, basic needs).
Verdict: Historic treaty promises outrank federal Fisheries Act regulations.

The Fatal Flaw: The Undefined Trap

  • The Court confirmed the right to a "moderate livelihood"... but never defined what that means!
  • 1999 Burnt Church Crisis (NB): DFO gunboats ram Mi'kmaw dories; lines cut; riot police deployed.
  • 2020 St. Mary's Bay (NS): Sipekne'katik harvests under Netukulimk; commercial fishers blockade wharves and burn a pound.
Because government never defined the rules, violence erupted on the water.

SYNTHESIS • CITIZENSHIP IN NOVA SCOTIA
“We Are All Treaty People”

What Non-Indigenous Citizens Receive

  • The Mi'kmaq never lost a war of conquest and never surrendered land.
  • The Peace Treaties are what allow non-Indigenous citizens to live, build homes, go to school, and own property peacefully in Nova Scotia today.
  • Without the 1760 treaty, non-Indigenous presence has no legal peace agreement.

What Non-Indigenous Citizens Owe

  • Holding Government Accountable: Demanding that Ottawa honor the promises made by the Crown.
  • Respecting Inherent Rights: Recognizing that treaty rights are constitutionally protected, not "special privileges."
  • Active Reconciliation: Learning the real history and protecting shared water and resources.
