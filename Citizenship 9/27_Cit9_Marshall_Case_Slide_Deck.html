The Encounter • Midnight, May 28, 1971

  • Two teenagers: Donald Marshall Jr. (17) meets friend Sandy Seale (17) in Wentworth Park.
  • The ambush: Roy Ebsary (58, armed with a knife) confronts them. Seale is fatally stabbed; Marshall is slashed defending him.
  • The response: Donald does not flee. He sprints straight to Sydney Police to report the killer and get medical help.
He did what any 17-year-old would do: he ran to the police for help.

He went to the police for protection. They put him in handcuffs.

ACT I • THE CONVICTION • NOVEMBER 1971
Tunnel Vision & Coerced Witnesses

Police Tunnel Vision

  • Detectives ignored every piece of evidence pointing to Roy Ebsary.
  • Police pressured and intimidated teenage witnesses into signing false statements claiming they saw Donald do it.
  • Authorities buried reports that proved Donald Marshall Jr.'s innocence.
Police decided who was guilty first, then fabricated evidence to fit their theory.

The All-White Courtroom

  • A 17-year-old Indigenous youth faced an all-white jury and an all-white court.
  • His defence lawyer failed to interview witnesses or challenge false police testimony.
  • The jury took just minutes to convict Donald of non-capital murder.
Sentenced to life imprisonment at age 17 for a crime he never committed.
FABRICATED CONVICTION The court convicted a 17-year-old boy based entirely on coerced lies and police tunnel vision.
ACT I • SPRINGHILL & DORCHESTER • 1971–1982
11 Years in Maximum Security

Stolen Youth Behind Bars

  • Sent to maximum-security federal penitentiaries (Springhill and Dorchester).
  • Ages 17 to 28: Marshall spent his entire youth locked in a cell for a murder he did not commit.
  • Endured extreme violence, racism, and solitary confinement, but never stopped maintaining his innocence.
The real killer walked free on the streets of Nova Scotia for over a decade.

The Truth Finally Breaks Out (1982)

  • In 1982, a key witness finally came forward and confessed: police pressured him to lie.
  • Roy Ebsary's own daughter went to police after hearing her father brag about the stabbing.
  • Marshall was finally released on bail in 1982 after 11 stolen years.
Exonerated only after outside confessions forced the system to admit its error.

ACT II • TRUTH & VINDICATION • 1989
1989: The Royal Commission Exposes Systemic Racism
Exoneration Archive • 1989
Press Conference (1989): Donald Marshall Jr. speaks to national media following his exoneration. Forced Canada's first full inquiry into institutional racism.

The Royal Commission Findings

  • Acquitted in 1983. Nova Scotia launched a landmark Royal Commission inquiry.
  • The Historic Verdict: Concluded the justice system failed Donald Marshall Jr. at every single step due to systemic racism.
  • Proved racism was built into the institutional procedures of police and courtrooms.
  • Roy Ebsary was finally tried and convicted of manslaughter.
Created the Mi'kmaw Legal Support Network (MLSN) and restorative justice in Nova Scotia.
SYSTEMIC REVELATION Racism wasn't just individual prejudice; it was built into the institutional procedures of the police and courts.
ACT II • NATIONAL LEGAL REFORM • 1999
Gladue Rights: Reforming Canadian Courts

The Gladue Principle (Criminal Code s. 718.2(e))

  • Born directly from the injustice suffered by Donald Marshall Jr.
  • Parliament amended the Criminal Code; Supreme Court affirmed in R. v. Gladue (1999).
  • Mandatory Law: Canadian judges *must* apply Gladue principles when sentencing any Indigenous person.
A permanent transformation in Canadian criminal law inspired by Marshall's struggle.

Two Mandatory Legal Duties for Judges

  • 1. Consider Systemic Background: Must evaluate the history of colonialism, residential schools, discrimination, and poverty.
  • 2. Seek Restorative Alternatives: Must actively look for community-based healing and alternatives before sending an Indigenous person to prison.
The law demands restorative healing rather than reflexive imprisonment.

ACT III • POMQUET HARBOUR, NS • AUGUST 1993
463 Pounds of Eels: The Federal Ambush

Fishing for Survival

  • Ten years after prison, Donald Marshall Jr. and his wife fish Pomquet Harbour in a small boat.
  • Caught 463 pounds of adult eels using traditional nets.
  • Sold the catch for $787.10 to buy groceries and pay rent.
  • Exercising ancient, inherent harvesting practices to feed his family.
Marshall was not running a corporate fleet; he was putting food on his family's table.

The Federal Ambush (DFO Swoop)

  • Department of Fisheries and Oceans (DFO) officers swoop in and seize his boat, nets, and eels.
  • 3 Federal Criminal Charges:
    • Fishing out of commercial season
    • Fishing without a federal licence
    • Using an illegal net
Under Canada's Fisheries Act, he was guilty. His defence? A 233-year-old treaty.

ACT IV • THE 1760 TREATY • PRIMARY EVIDENCE
The 233-Year-Old Piece of Parchment
“Saving only unto the said tribe the free liberty of Hunting & Fishing “as usual”... And that a “Truckhouse” shall be established at Fort Edward or elsewhere, for furnishing the Indians with “necessaries” in Exchange for their Peltry and other commodities.”
— Treaty of Peace and Friendship, Signed 1760 at Halifax / Fort Edward

The Three Vital Legal Promises:

PROMISE 1
“As Usual”
Mi'kmaw harvesting was recognized as an ancient, customary right—not a permit or handout granted by the British Crown.
PROMISE 2
The “Truckhouse”
A British Crown trading post established so the Mi'kmaq could sell their resources for everyday goods. Not a modern truck stop!
PROMISE 3
“Necessaries”
The right to trade for basic survival goods: food, clothing, and shelter (today: what courts call a “moderate livelihood”).

ACT IV • THE COURTROOM CLASH • 1993–1999
Modern Federal Law vs. Ancient Treaty

Crown Prosecutor & DFO (Federal Law)

  • Canada's Fisheries Act governs all Canadian waters and applies to all people equally.
  • No individual may fish out of season, without a licence, or sell catch commercially without a federal permit.
  • Treaties signed 233 years ago are historical relics, not modern commercial licences.
Under federal fisheries regulations, Donald Marshall Jr. was guilty on all three charges.

Donald Marshall Jr. & Defence (Treaty Law)

  • The 1760 Treaty is a sacred nation-to-nation covenant guaranteeing harvesting and trade rights.
  • Section 35 of Canada's 1982 Constitution recognized and affirmed treaty rights as supreme law.
  • A federal fisheries regulation cannot wipe out a constitutional treaty right!
Constitutional treaty rights outrank federal department regulations.
CONSTITUTIONAL CLASH Which law wins: Modern federal fisheries regulations, or a 233-year-old constitutional treaty promise?
ACT IV • THE TREATY OF 1760 • NATION-TO-NATION
Peace & Friendship — Not Conquest

What Is NOT in the Treaty

  • Zero Land Surrendered or Sold: There is no deed, purchase, or conquest clause in this treaty.
  • The Mi'kmaq never lost a war against the British Crown.
  • Unceded Territory: Nova Scotia and Mi'kma'ki remain legally unceded land.
This was a nation-to-nation commercial peace agreement, not a surrender.

Why Treaties Matter To Everyone Today

  • The British Crown needed peace with the Mi'kmaq to survive and settle in Nova Scotia.
  • In exchange for peace, the Crown legally guaranteed harvesting and trade protection.
  • Both nations signed as sovereign equals. Treaties are ongoing covenants that bind both sides.
Without these peace treaties, non-Indigenous settlement has no legal foundation in Mi'kma'ki.

ACT V • SUPREME COURT OF CANADA • SEPT 17, 1999
The Acquittal: Treaties Outrank Federal Law

The Supreme Court Ruling (1999)

  • Donald Marshall Jr. is ACQUITTED of all criminal charges!
  • Section 35 of Canada's Constitution (1982) recognizes and affirms Aboriginal and treaty rights as supreme law.
  • The Court rules the 1760 Treaty is alive and legally binding today.
Historic treaty promises outrank federal Fisheries Act regulations.

The "Moderate Livelihood" Right

  • Justice Ian Binnie ruled the Truckhouse clause protects an ongoing right to catch and trade fish for a “Moderate Livelihood”.
  • Defined as: Securing basic necessities—food, clothing, housing, and amenities.
  • First time in Canadian history that an Indigenous commercial harvesting right was upheld by the highest court.
Donald Marshall Jr. stood up twice against the justice system—and won both times.
CONSTITUTIONAL TRIUMPH The Supreme Court of Canada affirmed: Treaty rights are not historical artifacts; they are supreme constitutional law.
ACT V • THE FATAL FLAW & LIVE TALLY
The Fatal Flaw: The Undefined Dollar Trap

The Undefined Dollar Vacuum

  • The Court confirmed the right to a "moderate livelihood"... but never set a dollar figure, season limit, or boat quota!
  • Is a moderate livelihood $30,000? $50,000? $80,000? $100,000+?
  • Ottawa and DFO stalled and failed to negotiate clear regulations with Mi'kmaw communities.
Leaving the definition blank created a legal vacuum that both sides filled with conflict.

The Clashes: Burnt Church & St. Mary's Bay

  • 1999 Burnt Church (NB): DFO boats rammed Indigenous lobster skiffs; traps seized and destroyed.
  • 2020 St. Mary's Bay (NS): Sipekne'katik launched its own treaty fishery under *Netukulimk*. Non-Indigenous commercial fishers blockaded wharves and burned a lobster pound.
Ambiguity in law produces conflict on the water.

ACT VI • CIVIC SYNTHESIS • ROOM 8
“We Are All Treaty People”

What Citizens Living in Mi'kma'ki Receive

  • The Mi'kmaq never lost a war of conquest and never surrendered land.
  • The Peace Treaties are what allow non-Indigenous citizens to live, build homes, go to school, and own property peacefully in Nova Scotia today.
  • Without the 1760 treaty, non-Indigenous presence has no legal peace agreement.
Treaties are not special privileges for Indigenous people; they are the peace pact for all of us.

What Treaty Partners Owe

  • Holding Government Accountable: Demanding that Ottawa honor Crown treaty promises rather than stalling.
  • Respecting Inherent Rights: Recognizing that treaty harvesting rights are constitutionally protected supreme law.
  • Active Reconciliation: Learning the real history and protecting shared lands, waters, and resources.
Reconciliation is an active civic duty, not an optional gesture.
