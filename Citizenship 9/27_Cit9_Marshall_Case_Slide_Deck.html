ACT I • THE BUST • AUGUST 1993
Pomquet Harbour, Nova Scotia: 463 Pounds of Eels

The Man: Donald Marshall Jr.

  • Mi'kmaw man from Membertou First Nation.
  • Wrongful Conviction: At age 17, sentenced to life in prison for a murder he did not commit.
  • Spent 11 years in maximum security before being fully exonerated with a royal commission and apology.
August 1993: Marshall and his wife go out in a small boat on Pomquet Harbour to catch eels for food and rent money.

The Federal Ambush

  • Sold 463 lbs of eels for $787.10 to buy groceries and shelter.
  • Federal DFO fisheries officers swarm, seize his nets and catch.
  • Charged with 3 Federal Crimes:
    • Fishing out of commercial season
    • Fishing without a federal license
    • Using an illegal net
Under Canada's Fisheries Act, he is 100% guilty. No debate.
ROOM QUESTION If Canadian federal law says he is guilty, how on earth did Donald Marshall Jr. walk into court and claim he had done nothing wrong?
ACT II • THE DEFENSE • 1760 AT FORT EDWARD
The 233-Year-Old Piece of Parchment
“Saving only unto the said tribe the free liberty of Hunting & Fishing as usual... And that a Truckhouse shall be established at Fort Edward or elsewhere, for furnishing the Indians with necessaries in Exchange for their Peltry and other commodities.”
— Treaty of Peace and Friendship, Signed 1760 at Windsor / Fort Edward

What The King Promised in 1760

  • The Mi'kmaq retain free hunting and fishing “as usual” (no royal permits or seasons).
  • The British promise a Truckhouse (trading post) to exchange resources for necessaries (food, clothing, shelter).

What Is NOT in the Treaty

  • There is zero land surrendered or sold.
  • Nova Scotia and Mi'kma'ki are unceded territory.
  • This was a nation-to-nation peace pact, not a conquest.
THE LEGAL CLASH Donald Marshall's lawyer argued: "A promise made by the King in 1760 is constitutional law. It outranks a federal fishing regulation." Can an ancient treaty beat modern law?
ACT III • THE SUPREME COURT RULING • SEPTEMBER 1999
The Acquittal — And The Explosion at Burnt Church

The Supreme Court Ruling (1999)

  • Donald Marshall Jr. is ACQUITTED of all charges.
  • Section 35 of Canada's Constitution (1982) protects historic treaties as supreme law.
  • The Truckhouse clause means Mi'kmaw people have an inherent right to catch fish and trade them for a “Moderate Livelihood” (food, shelter, basic needs).
Verdict: Historic treaty promises outrank federal Fisheries Act regulations.

The Fatal Flaw: The Blank Check

  • The Supreme Court confirmed the right to a “moderate livelihood”... but never defined what that means!
  • Is it $40,000/yr? $80,000/yr? How many lobster traps?
  • October 1999 • Burnt Church Crisis: Mi'kmaq drop traps; non-Indigenous commercial fishers destroy them; federal DFO gunboats ram native dories on Miramichi Bay.
Because Ottawa never defined the rules, violence erupted on the water.
DISCUSSION POINT Why does leaving "moderate livelihood" undefined guarantee that commercial fishers and treaty harvesters will end up in a brawl on the wharf?
ACT IV • ST. MARY'S BAY • SEPTEMBER 2020
21 Years Later: The Exact Same Standoff Re-Ignites

Worldview A: Netukulimk

Sipekne'katik First Nation • Saulnierville Wharf, NS

  • Inherent Authority: Rights passed down from ancestors, protected by 1760 treaty.
  • Netukulimk: Taking only what is needed for community sustenance without compromising future generations.
  • Community launches its own self-regulated livelihood fishery outside federal commercial season.

Worldview B: Crown Regulation

Department of Fisheries & Oceans (DFO) • Commercial Fleets

  • State Jurisdiction: Federal government holds authority over oceans and fisheries.
  • Conservation: Closed seasons and strict trap limits are designed to protect lobsters during molting/breeding.
  • Belief that one single rulebook and calendar must apply to everyone on the water.
THE FLASHPOINT In 2020, commercial fishers blockaded ports, flares were fired, and a lobster facility was burned down. Who has the legal right to set the rules on this water?
SYNTHESIS • CITIZENSHIP IN NOVA SCOTIA
“We Are All Treaty People”

What Non-Indigenous Citizens Receive

  • The Mi'kmaq never lost a war of conquest and never surrendered land.
  • The Peace Treaties are what allow non-Indigenous citizens to live, build homes, go to school, and own property peacefully in Nova Scotia today.
  • Without the 1760 treaty, non-Indigenous presence has no legal peace agreement.

What Non-Indigenous Citizens Owe

  • Holding Government Accountable: Demanding that Ottawa honor the promises made by the Crown.
  • Respecting Inherent Rights: Recognizing that treaty rights are constitutionally protected, not "special privileges."
  • Active Reconciliation: Learning the real history and protecting shared water and resources.
