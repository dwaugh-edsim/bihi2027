The Canadian Debate — Public vs Private Funded Care

Healthy Living 9 · O3 Health Literacy · Structured debate brief (Classes 9–13)

O3: Learners will investigate ways to access health resources that support health and well-being. This brief investigates a core access question: who gets care first — everyone equally, or those who can pay? Cross-reference: CIT9 Unit 5A (issues & values in governance).

PAGE 1 · THE GROUND RULES
The debate question Should Canadians be allowed to pay money for faster private medical care — or does that dilute the public system everyone shares?

THE RULES WE ALREADY PLAY BY (Canada Health Act, 1984)

  • Five principles: public administration, comprehensiveness, universality, portability, accessibility — medically necessary hospital and doctor care is meant to be available to everyone, free at the point of care.
  • The Act also deters extra-billing and user fees for insured services (provinces can lose federal health transfer money). That is why you cannot legally be charged at the door for a medically necessary doctor/hospital visit in Canada.
  • But private pay already exists for services outside the Act’s core: dental, prescription drugs, physiotherapy, vision, psychologists, and private virtual care (e.g., paid apps like Maple). That is the existing “two-tier” reality.

COURT ANCHORS (what Canadian courts have said)

  • Chaoulli v. Quebec (Supreme Court of Canada, 2005): Quebec’s ban on private insurance for medically necessary care was struck down (6–3) under the Quebec Charter — because long public waits were violating the right to life/security of the person. It did not legalize private care everywhere; it opened the door.
  • Cambie Surgeries v. British Columbia: a private clinic challenged BC’s restrictions on private billing; the BC Court of Appeal upheld the restrictions (2022) and the Supreme Court of Canada declined to hear the appeal (2023). So today, the Canada Health Act’s no-extra-billing rule still stands.

THE WAIT-TIME PRESSURE (why the debate exists)

  • Fraser Institute’s Waiting Your Turn (2024): median waits for some surgeries are very long (e.g., orthopaedic ~57 weeks, neurosurgery ~46 weeks in that report’s data). Long waits are the main argument FOR allowing private options.
  • Compare the US (exhibit 67): fast access if you can pay — but ~26M uninsured and medical debt bankruptcies. That is the cautionary tale AGAINST a US-style market.
  • Other OECD countries (France, Germany, Australia) run mixed systems: strong public core + regulated private options. The debate is really “which mixed model, if any?”

The Debate — Pros, Cons, and Your Worksheet

Healthy Living 9 · O3 · structured pros/cons (claim → evidence → reasoning)

O3: Investigate ways to access health resources. Rule: argue from public facts and fictional examples only — never from your own or your family’s health experiences.

PAGE 2 · PROS / CONS / WORKSHEET

ARGUMENTS FOR ALLOWING MORE PRIVATE PAY

  • Choice and speed for those who can pay — less pain, faster return to work/school.
  • Keeps care (and money) in Canada instead of Canadians travelling abroad for surgery.
  • Can fund extra capacity (clinics, equipment) that the public system can also use.
  • Other wealthy democracies run mixed systems without collapsing the public core.

ARGUMENTS AGAINST (the “dilution” case)

  • Access by wallet, not need — contradicts universality/accessibility; the sickest are often the poorest.
  • Siphons staff and time from public hospitals → longer public waits for everyone else (studies of mixed systems find mixed results, but the risk is real).
  • Erodes the no-extra-billing rule the Canada Health Act is built on.
  • Insurance overhead and medical debt — the US shows where a market-led system ends up (exhibit 67).
  • Most Canadians polled oppose two-tier care for medically necessary services.

DISCUSSION QUESTIONS

  • Where is the line? Should ER care, cancer treatment, or childbirth EVER be for sale? What does the Canada Health Act say about that?
  • We already pay privately for dental, drugs, and physio. Is that a two-tier system too — and is it fair?
  • If private clinics reduce public wait lists (the Fraser data), is that worth the dilution risk? What evidence would convince you?
  • What would a “mixed model with a strong public core” (France/Germany/Australia style) look like in Nova Scotia?
