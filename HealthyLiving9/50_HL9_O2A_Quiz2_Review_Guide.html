1STBBI BIOLOGY

What causes what, how it moves, and what testing can honestly tell you.

The bacterial / viral split

Bacterial STBBIs (chlamydia, gonorrhea, syphilis) can be cured with antibiotics. Viral ones (HIV, herpes, HPV, hepatitis B) cannot be cured, but they can be managed and treated. Some are curable, some are manageable.

Vaccines exist for HPV and hepatitis B. They are prevention, not treatment: they train your immune system before exposure and cannot cure an infection someone already has. There is no vaccine for HIV, chlamydia, gonorrhea or syphilis.

HIV today: effective treatment, available since 1996, means people with HIV live about as long as anyone else. When treatment reaches an undetectable viral load, HIV cannot pass on through sex.

Asymptomatic infections

Most STBBIs are asymptomatic much of the time: up to 7 in 10 women and about half of men with chlamydia never notice a symptom. You cannot tell by looking at someone. Symptoms, when they exist, can also mislead: a painless sore that heals by itself can be syphilis, where the sore goes but the infection stays (which is why syphilis is called “the great imitator”). Herpes sheds between sores, so “healed” does not mean harmless.

Takeaway: testing is the only way to know your status.

How STBBIs move, and how they do not

  • Transmission requires sexual contact: genital skin-to-skin or body-fluid exchange. A toilet seat does neither.
  • Oral sex removes the pregnancy risk but only reduces without eliminating STI risk: chlamydia, gonorrhea, herpes and hepatitis B are all possible. Condoms and dental dams reduce it further.
  • HIV passes only in specific fluids: blood, semen, vaginal and rectal fluids, and breast milk. Not in saliva, sweat, tears or pee.
  • Shared razors can carry traces of blood too small to see, and bloodborne viruses (hepatitis B and C, HIV) can move that way. Razors are single-person items.

What testing actually is

Most STI testing is a pee cup and a blood draw, not a swab. Testing is opt-in: no routine check-up sweeps for it automatically, so you ask for it by name.

A clean result is a snapshot of the past. It does not cover exposures that happened after the test, and routine panels usually leave out herpes and HPV. Being in a committed relationship is not a substitute: testing is about what happened before you got together, people can carry an infection for years without knowing, and faithfulness only protects you if it is mutual.

2CONTRACEPTION & PROTECTION

What each method does, how well it works in practice, and what it does not cover.

The method families

  • Hormonal (pill, patch, ring, injection, implant): prevent pregnancy. No STI protection at all.
  • IUDs (hormonal and copper): long-acting, placed by a provider. The copper IUD is also the most effective form of emergency contraception if placed within 7 days.
  • Emergency contraception (morning-after pill): works best within 24 hours and gets less effective the longer you wait, but it does still work later. “Sooner is better” does not mean “point of no return.”
  • Barriers (external and internal condoms, dental dams): the only family that also reduces STI risk.

Effectiveness: perfect use vs typical use

Effectiveness numbers come in two versions. Perfect use is measured under controlled conditions; typical use is real life. For condoms: 98% with perfect use, about 87% with typical use. Fit, timing and expiry are what separate the two.

Condom facts that change the number

  • Two condoms at once give less protection, not more. Friction between the layers makes them tear, slip or bunch.
  • Storage matters. A wallet is the worst place a condom lives: body heat, friction and time weaken latex. Check the expiry date; keep spares somewhere cool, flat and unused.
  • Withdrawal is not a method to rely on: pre-ejaculate can contain live sperm, and it gives zero STI protection.

Dual protection

Condom plus another method is called dual protection, because pregnancy and STIs are different risks covered by different tools. The pill, patch, ring, injection, implant and IUD all prevent pregnancy only. The condom is what adds the STI layer.

Side effects

Effects depend on the method: there is no strong link between the pill or patch and weight gain. The Depo injection is the exception, associated with real weight gain. Beyond that, it depends on the person and on what else is changing at the same time.

3CONSENT

The definition, what disqualifies it, and the law around age.

The definition

Consent must be informed, sober, ongoing and freely given, and it is reversible: it can be withdrawn at any moment, for any reason, with no explanation owed. It has to be active and affirmative: a real yes.

What is not consent

A person who freezes, stays silent, goes along with it, or is unable to respond is not consenting. Only a clear, active yes counts.

Alcohol and degrees

Impairment runs on a spectrum, and the more impaired a person is, the less able they are to consent. When someone is too drunk to know what they are agreeing to, there is no consent, and the sober person is the one responsible for checking.

Assault by someone known

Statistics Canada found that about three in four people who were sexually assaulted knew the person: a friend, an acquaintance, a partner, or a family member. A stranger in a dark alley is the exception, not the rule.

Age and the law

The age of consent in Canada is 16, with a close-in-age exemption: for 14- and 15-year-olds, the partner must be less than five years older. Whether a specific pair falls inside that exemption can depend on how far apart their birthdays are. A large age gap in the teen years can also involve a power imbalance.

4ACCESS & THE LAW IN NOVA SCOTIA

What you can arrange yourself, where, and what you have to ask for by name.
  • In NS there is no parent permission and no minimum age to get tested for an STI, get birth control, or get a pregnancy test.
  • The mature-minor rule: once a provider judges that you understand the treatment, you consent to it yourself, and the care stays confidential.
  • Where to go: a Youth Health Centre, a public health unit, a family doctor, or call 811.
  • The Halifax Sexual Health Centre is a full-service clinic: pills, rings, patches, shots, IUDs, implants and emergency contraception, plus testing and information. Condoms are the smallest part of what they do.
  • STI testing is opt-in: ask for it by name, because no routine check-up includes it automatically.
