Crossing the Border - The US Health System

Healthy Living 9 · O3 Health Literacy · Exhibit (Classes 9-13)

O3: Learners will investigate ways to access health resources that support health and well-being - including how health systems differ, and what that means for you as a Canadian traveller.

PAGE 1 · THE SYSTEM
The one rule that matters Your Nova Scotia health card (MSI) is meaningless in the US. Government of Canada (travel.gc.ca): provincial plans cover little or none of medical costs outside Canada, cap any emergency coverage at what the care would cost in NS, and never pay up front. US hospitals can demand immediate payment. Buy travel medical insurance before you go.

HOW THE US SYSTEM WORKS (60 seconds)

  • Most Americans get health insurance through an employer or buy a “plan”. The rest rely on government programs (Medicare 65+, Medicaid low-income) - or have no insurance.
  • HMO plans: lower cost, but you must use in-network providers and usually need a referral from your primary doctor to see a specialist.
  • PPO plans: more choice (out-of-network allowed at higher cost), often use coinsurance - you pay a percentage of the bill after the deductible.
  • Key words: premium (monthly fee) · deductible (you pay this first) · copay (flat fee per visit) · coinsurance (% you pay) · out-of-pocket maximum (the cap) · in-network vs. out-of-network.

THE TWO SIDES OF THE US SYSTEM

  • If you can afford it: excellent, fast care. Insured Americans often get specialist appointments and elective surgery weeks faster than Canadians waiting on our lists.
  • If you can’t: tens of millions of Americans are uninsured (about 26 million in 2024), and medical debt affects a large share of adults - it is a leading cause of personal bankruptcy in the US. Many delay or skip care entirely.
  • EMTALA: a US ER must screen and stabilize anyone who comes in, regardless of ability to pay or insurance - but it is not free care; the bill arrives afterwards.
  • Waits: the US has ER waits too. “Boarding” means waiting in the ER for a hospital bed (JAMA 2026: over half of admitted patients waited 4+ hours). “No lineups” is only true for people with good insurance and non-urgent problems.

Crossing the Border - Travel Insurance Checklist & Scenarios

O3: Investigate ways to access health resources - what to check BEFORE you travel, and how a medical bill across the border actually works.

PAGE 2 · BEFORE YOU GO

TRAVEL MEDICAL INSURANCE - READ THE FINE PRINT

  • Coverage limit: look for $1,000,000+ (or unlimited) per person.
  • Covers: emergency medical + hospital, ambulance, and medical evacuation / repatriation (getting you flown or driven back to Canada for care).
  • Pre-existing conditions: usually covered only if stable for a set period (often 90 days) - disclose honestly or the whole policy can be void.
  • Activities: skiing, sports, e-bikes? Some policies exclude them unless you add a rider.
  • Who pays first: in the US, you usually pay up front and claim afterwards - keep every receipt, report within the deadline (often 24-90 days), and call the 24/7 assistance line before big decisions (they can direct you to a partner hospital).
  • Timing: buy when you book the trip if you want “cancel for any reason” coverage.
  • School trips: check whether the school board’s trip policy already covers you - then read what it covers. Your parents’ employer or credit card may also include travel medical insurance.

FICTIONAL SCENARIOS - what would you do?

A - The Boston ear infection

On a school trip to Boston, Jamie (14) gets a painful ear infection at 9 p.m. The trip bought travel insurance. Options: call the insurer’s 24/7 line and go to the recommended urgent care (pay by card, keep receipts, claim later) · flash the NS health card at the front desk · wait two days until back in NS. Which works, and why?

B - No insurance in Maine

Sam (15) is visiting family in Maine with NO travel insurance and breaks a wrist skateboarding. The ER will stabilize it under EMTALA - but then what? Roughly what bill could arrive, and what could the family do (payment plans, financial aid/“charity care” programs many US hospitals offer, negotiate the bill)?

C - The comparison

Cousin Alex in Ohio gets an MRI in 3 days with insurance; the same non-urgent MRI in NS can mean a long wait. Why the difference? What does each system trade off? (One sentence each.)
