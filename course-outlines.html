Grade 9 · Social Studies

Citizenship 9

Nova Scotia Citizenship curriculum · Three sections (901, 902, 903)

Citizenship 9 is built around one deceptively simple question: what does it actually take to take part in the place you live? Students start with their own identities and communities, then work outward through how government really works in Nova Scotia and Canada, how economic and media systems shape everyday life, and how the rights people hold were won, lost, and won back. Because Nova Scotia is Mi'kma'ki, the treaty relationship between the Crown and the Mi'kmaq — and the history and present-day governance of Indigenous peoples in this province — runs through the entire year rather than sitting in a single unit. Truth and reconciliation is treated here as unfinished civic work, not a chapter that closes.

The course is hands-on and discussion-heavy. Students argue from evidence in structured debates and caucuses, work through case studies and simulations, analyse budgets and news coverage, take on a sustained community engagement project, and finish the year with substantial independent research on a global issue. Some of the history we study is genuinely difficult, and it is taught with care, clear ground rules, and room for students to step back when they need to. The goal by June is a student who can read a headline, trace an issue to the right level of government, weigh evidence honestly, and speak up with confidence.

General Themes

  • Civic identity, worldview, and belonging
  • Rights and responsibilities across a lifetime
  • Mi'kma'ki, Peace and Friendship Treaties, and reconciliation
  • How government works — federal, provincial, and municipal
  • Economic citizenship: taxation, budgets, fairness and equity
  • Digital and media citizenship
  • Governance, elections, and democratic engagement
  • Advocacy, protest, and making change
  • Community engagement and service learning
  • Global citizenship and human rights

Curriculum Outcomes

  1. Learners will evaluate evolving concepts and attributes of citizenship as members of different kinds of communities. Civic identity · worldview · rights and responsibilities
  2. Learners will evaluate key changes in the citizenship rights of Mi'kmaw and other traditionally disempowered people. Treaties · the Indian Act · residential schools · the Sixties Scoop · the TRC's Calls to Action · reconciliation
  3. Learners will investigate the structure, operation, and selection of government in Canada, including federal, provincial, territorial, indigenous, and municipal government models. Levels of government · jurisdiction · elections · Indigenous and non-Indigenous governance
  4. Learners will analyse how issues become valued within and across all areas of government and society. Values and worldview · political parties and platforms · Canadian identity
  5. Learners will evaluate strategies to meaningfully engage as citizens within a democratic process. Advocacy · civic action · protest and civil disobedience · unequal access to democracy
  6. Learners will compare the impact of economic decisions from the perspectives of various groups of people. Needs, wants, and value · taxation · economic disparity · fairness, equality, and equity
  7. Learners will analyse how effective decision-making and informed participation in society affect, and are affected by, personal financial management. Budgeting · saving · credit and debt · financial goals · consumer choices
  8. Learners will evaluate how perceptions of current issues are influenced by various media, and how this shapes actions, choices, and reactions. Media representation and omission · agenda-setting · current events
  9. Learners will evaluate risks, rights, and responsibilities of digital citizens. Digital footprint · privacy · anonymity · online accountability
  10. Learners will evaluate the consequences of action and inaction as twenty-first century global citizens. Globalization · human rights and the UDHR · Canada's response to global issues
  11. Learners will construct a collaboratively-designed service learning project which addresses a need in the school or larger community. Community need · planning · implementation · reflection on citizenship growth
Grade 9 · Health

Healthy Living 9

Nova Scotia Healthy Living curriculum · Three sections (901, 902, 903)

Healthy Living 9 looks honestly at the decisions teenagers really face — the ones that don't wait until they're ready. Over the year students work through how sleep, screens, and the attention economy shape their brains and bodies; how to find reliable health information and actually navigate clinics and services; how to communicate, refuse, and repair in relationships; how gender norms and power show up around them; how to stay safe online and offline; and how substances, addiction, stress, and mental health work, and where help lives when any of it gets heavy. The course also names the elephant in the room: this is the year high school starts arriving, so questions about credits, transcripts, and how to ask a teacher for help are part of the curriculum too.

Expect real data, real scenarios, and straight answers. Students analyse their own habits and the systems designed to capture their attention, practise the actual words for hard conversations, and build concrete resources — where to go, who to talk to, what to say. Sensitive topics are taught with established ground rules, anonymous ways to ask questions, and the option to step back; nothing important is taught through surprise. Families who would like to preview material or ask what's coming up are always welcome to make contact.

General Themes

  • Health behaviours for the short and long term
  • Sleep, screens, and the attention economy
  • Sexual and reproductive health
  • Health literacy and navigating the healthcare system
  • Communication skills for healthy relationships
  • Gender norms, stereotypes, and equality
  • Gender-based violence: recognition and response
  • Personal safety and injury prevention, online and offline
  • Bystander intervention
  • Navigating change, resilience, rest, and relaxation
  • Addiction and substance use
  • Help-seeking and mental health support
  • The transition to high school

Curriculum Outcomes

  1. Learners will evaluate health behaviours that promote short-term and long-term health Healthy sleep · technology use · disease prevention · identity and health-related decisions
  2. Learners will analyse ways to care for one's own sexual and reproductive health Accurate and reliable information · options, supports, and treatments · decision-making factors
  3. Learners will investigate ways to access health resources that support health and well-being Health literacy · accessing information and care · navigating the healthcare system
  4. Learners will develop communication skills that support the growth and maintenance of healthy relationships Expressing needs and disagreement · assertiveness, negotiation, and refusal · conflict resolution · impacts of language
  5. Learners will reflect on conditions that positively counter harmful gender norms, stereotypes, and biases within relationships Conditions influencing gender norms · power dynamics · promoting gender equality
  6. Learners will evaluate responses to gender-based violence Contributing factors · countering harmful behaviours · supporting self and others · strategies for help
  7. Learners will investigate ways to care for one's personal safety and ways to prevent injury online and offline Injury risk factors · rights and responsibilities online · online victimization · personal boundaries
  8. Learners will develop bystander intervention skills across a variety of emergency and risk scenarios Emergencies and risks · taking action · overcoming barriers to action
  9. Learners will evaluate healthy ways to navigate change and/or challenging life circumstances Protective factors · coping with life challenges · practices for rest, fun, and relaxation
  10. Learners will evaluate the impact of addiction on physical, mental, emotional, spiritual health Protective and enabling influences · impacts of various kinds of addictions · impacts of substance use
  11. Learners will develop help-seeking efficacy skills Signs of concern · communication skills · community resources for help
Grade 8 · Health

Healthy Living 8

Nova Scotia Healthy Living curriculum · Four sections (801, 802, 803, 804)

Healthy Living 8 gives students a working model of their own health that they can actually use. The organizing idea for the year is that health is a system: physical, mental and emotional, social, spiritual, and environmental well-being are connected, and the habits forming right now — how they sleep, eat, move, manage stress, treat each other, and spend time online — pull on all five at once. From that foundation the course moves through life skills and decision-making, health behaviours, mental health literacy, growth and sexual health, relationships and safety, and the pressures around substances. It is the health course that builds the vocabulary and the habits the later years assume.

Grade eight is a year of new pressures — harder friendships, louder online lives, bigger feelings — so the classroom is designed to be a place where those can be looked at directly and without embarrassment. Learning is active: students map their own habits, work through scenarios and case studies, discuss and role-play, and hear factual answers to the questions they actually have. Sensitive material comes with clear ground rules, anonymous question options, and the chance to step back. The aim is simple: students leave grade eight knowing how their health works, how to make a decision under pressure, and how to find help when they or a friend need it.

General Themes

  • The five dimensions of health as an interconnected system
  • Life skills: decision-making, coping, and interpersonal skills
  • Health behaviours: food, sleep, activity, and technology
  • Communicable disease prevention
  • Mental health literacy: the brain, stress, anxiety, and stigma
  • Help-seeking
  • Adolescent growth and development
  • Sexual and reproductive health and reliable information
  • Body image
  • Healthy relationships and violence prevention
  • Substance misuse prevention and social pressure

Curriculum Outcomes

  1. Learners will analyse how life skills influence physical, mental, emotional, social, and spiritual health Healthy decision-making · healthy ways of coping · interpersonal skills that enhance health
  2. Learners will analyse the relationships between health behaviours and physical, mental, emotional, social, and spiritual health Food choices and eating habits · sleep and health · physical activity · healthy behaviours for technology · communicable disease prevention
  3. Learners will reflect on the importance of mental health literacy Function of the brain · stress and anxiety · mental health stigma · help seeking
  4. Learners will evaluate ways to access reliable sexual health information that positively impacts adolescent sexual and reproductive health Preventing unintended pregnancy and STBBIs · adolescent growth and development · body image · sexual violence prevention
  5. Learners will reflect on how relationships impact physical, mental, emotional, social, and spiritual health Addressing challenges in relationships · safe relationships · recognizing healthy and unhealthy relationships · coercive behaviours · respecting the rights of others
  6. Learners will reflect on the impact of substance misuse on adolescent health Social influences and pressures · coping with pressures and difficult emotions · injury related to substance misuse · harms of use and co-use

This outline describes what students will learn, not the specific day-to-day work used to get there — particular activities, projects, and assessments may change during the year as the course responds to real events and to students. The outcome statements above are the published Nova Scotia curriculum outcomes; classroom assessment blends tests and quizzes, projects and presentations, simulations and discussions, and ongoing daily work. Questions about a specific class are best answered by the teacher.
