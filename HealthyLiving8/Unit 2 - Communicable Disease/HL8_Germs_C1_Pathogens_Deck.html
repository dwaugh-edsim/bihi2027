What makes you sick?

Three questions today. What is a germ? What kinds are there? How do they travel from one person to the next?

By the end of this class you can do one thing: look at any sickness and say what kind of germ it likely is, and how it probably travelled. That exact skill is on the quiz.

No notes needed today. The study sheet has everything.

Word One

Germ. That's the everyday word.

The science word is pathogen (say it: PATH-oh-jen). A pathogen is anything too small to see that can get into your body and make you sick.

Sickness you can catch from other people is called communicable (say it: kuh-MYOO-nih-kul). It just means catchable. A cold is communicable. A broken arm is not.

Two kinds of germ do almost all the damage: bacteria and viruses. Next two slides.
Kind One

Bacteria: one tiny cell, alive

The deal with bacteria
say it: back-TEER-ee-uh
  • One single cell. Alive, eating, growing, in a puddle or in your gut.
  • Most are harmless. Many are helpful. Trillions live in your stomach and help you digest food. Yogurt is made by bacteria.
  • A few kinds make you sick by multiplying fast or spraying toxins (poisons they make).
Sickness they cause
examples to know
  • Strep throat
  • Some ear infections
  • Food poisoning (Salmonella, E. coli, say it: sal-muh-NEH-luh, EE-co-lye)
  • Some skin infections
Doctors kill bacteria with antibiotics. Write that down for Class 4: antibiotics work on bacteria.
Kind Two

Viruses: instructions in a shell

The deal with viruses
say it: VY-russ-ez
  • Not really alive. No eating, no growing. Just a set of instructions inside a shell.
  • Way smaller than bacteria. A thousand viruses could line up across this period .
  • They break into your cells and turn each one into a virus-copying machine. That is why you feel awful.
Sickness they cause
examples to know
  • Colds
  • Flu
  • COVID-19
  • Chickenpox
  • Measles
Antibiotics do NOT kill viruses. Nothing you can buy kills them. Your immune system has to do the job. That is Class 3.

Rule of thumb for the quiz: stomach-and-skin trouble is usually bacteria. Coughs, sneezes, and spots are usually viruses. The food-poisoning family is bacteria.

How Germs Travel · Learn These Four

The four travel routes

1
Through the air
Tiny wet drops fly out when someone coughs, sneezes, or even talks. You breathe them in. (Science word: droplets.)
TRAVELS: colds, flu, COVID-19, measles
2
By touch
Hands, shared stuff, skin to skin. A germ sits on a doorknob or a desk, you touch it, then touch your face.
TRAVELS: pink eye, warts, norovirus, impetigo
3
In food and water
Undercooked meat, unwashed hands in food, unsafe water. The germ rides down your throat.
TRAVELS: Salmonella, E. coli, norovirus
4
By bite
A biting bug acts like a taxi. It bites something infected, picks up the germ, then bites you. (Science word: vector.)
TRAVELS: Lyme disease (ticks, real in Nova Scotia), West Nile (mosquitoes)

One germ can use more than one route. Norovirus (the vomiting bug) does air, touch, AND food. That is why it rips through a whole school.

The Other Half of the Story

Germs also need a way in

Travelling is not enough. A germ must get inside: your mouth, your nose, your eyes, or a cut in your skin. Your skin is a wall. Germs are always knocking.

And that is why your hands matter more than anything: hands touch the doorknob (route 2), then touch your face (the way in). Same two hands, both jobs.
