Word One

Germ. That's the everyday word.

The science word is pathogen (say it: PATH-oh-jen). A pathogen is anything too small to see that can get into your body and make you sick.

Sickness you can catch from other people is called communicable (say it: kuh-MYOO-nih-kul). It just means catchable. A cold is communicable. A broken arm is not.

Four kinds of germ do the damage: bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites. Our course focuses on the first two, because they cause almost every sickness you will catch this year.
SOURCE: HL8 course study content, Unit 2 (communicable disease definitions)
Kind One

Bacteria: one tiny cell, alive

The deal with bacteria
say it: back-TEER-ee-uh
  • One single cell. Alive, eating, growing, in a puddle or in your gut.
  • Most are harmless. Many are helpful. Trillions live in your stomach and help you digest food. Yogurt is made by bacteria.
  • A few kinds make you sick by multiplying fast or spraying toxins (poisons they make).
Sickness they cause
examples to know
  • Strep throat
  • Some ear infections
  • Food poisoning (Salmonella, E. coli)
  • Some skin infections
Doctors kill bacteria with antibiotics. Hold that thought. There is a whole slide coming on why that does NOT help a cold.
SOURCE: HL8 course study content, Unit 2 · Public Health Agency of Canada (canada.ca, food poisoning pages)
Kind Two · Living Cell vs Genetic Shell

Viruses: instructions in a shell

The deal with viruses
say it: VY-russ-ez
  • Not really alive. No eating, no growing. Just a set of instructions inside a shell.
  • Way smaller than bacteria. A thousand viruses could line up across this period .
  • They break into your cells and turn each one into a virus-copying machine. That is why you feel awful.
Sickness they cause
examples to know

Colds · Flu · COVID-19 · Chickenpox · Measles

The 1928 Accidental Discovery

Alexander Fleming & the first antibiotic

In 1928, scientist Alexander Fleming left a culture dish of staph bacteria open by a window. Mold grew on it—and around the mold, the bacteria died.
The mold was secreting a chemical weapon that destroyed bacterial cells. Fleming isolated it and named it penicillin—the world's very first antibiotic. It changed medicine forever.
How it kills bacteria

Antibiotics attack the parts a living cell needs to survive: its cell wall and internal factories. Bacteria are cells, so antibiotics wreck them.

Fleming's 1928 Culture Plate: Notice the clear halo where mold killed the bacteria.
SOURCE: Standard medical science history · Fleming 1928 discovery of penicillin
The Question Everyone Asks

Why doesn't penicillin fix a cold?

Why it works on bacteria
the machinery it attacks

Antibiotics attack the parts a living cell needs: the cell wall and the factories inside it. Bacteria are living cells, so antibiotics wreck them.

Why it fails on a cold
nothing to attack

A cold is a virus. A virus has NO cell wall, NO factories, and NO metabolism. It is instructions in a shell, hiding inside YOUR cells. There is literally nothing for the antibiotic to hit.

Taking antibiotics for a cold is a triple loss:
1. It does nothing to the cold (viruses don't care about antibiotics).
2. It kills your helpful gut bacteria (can give you stomach pain).
3. The surviving bacteria get tougher (creates antibiotic resistance — full story in Class 4).
SOURCE: Public Health Agency of Canada (canada.ca, antibiotic use and resistance pages)

The Five Routes · Deep Dive

The five travel routes · what to know

1
Airborne
Tiny wet drops fly when someone coughs, sneezes, or talks (science word: droplets). You breathe them in.
COLDS · FLU · COVID-19 · MEASLES
2
Direct contact
Skin to skin, person to person. Touching, high-fives, kissing, shaking hands.
PINK EYE · WARTS · IMPETIGO
3
Indirect contact
The germ waits on a surface. Doorknob, desk, phone, keyboard, pink chicken. You touch it, then touch your face.
NOROVIRUS · FOOD POISONING (Salmonella, E. COLI)
4
Bodily fluids
Blood and spit carry germs. Sharing drinks, water bottles, straws, or toothbrushes moves fluid between people.
MONO (THE KISSING DISEASE, IN SPIT) · HEPATITIS B
5
Vectors
A biting bug acts like a taxi. It bites an infected animal, picks up the germ, then bites you (science word: vector).
LYME DISEASE FROM TICKS · WEST NILE FROM MOSQUITOES

The Other Half of the Story

Germs also need a way in

Travelling is not enough. A germ must get inside: your mouth, your nose, your eyes, or a cut in your skin. Your skin is a wall. Germs are always knocking.

And that is why your hands matter more than anything: hands touch the doorknob (route 3), then touch your face (the way in). Same two hands, both jobs.
