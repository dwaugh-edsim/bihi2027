Part 1 · The Investigation Words (New Since Study Sheet 1) DEFINITIONS
Outbreak more sickness than expected
Many people getting the same sickness in the same place at about the same time.
Case 55: 3 sick kids Monday became 55 by Friday at Centennial Jr High.
Fomite say: FOH-mite
An object that carries germs from one person to the next.
Examples: traded resin ducks, keyboards, fountain taps, doorknobs, desks.
Incubation Period say: in-kyoo-BAY-shun
The gap between picking up a germ and feeling sick.
Norovirus: about 12 to 48 hours. Jayden: sick by night after a morning drink.
Hypothesis say: hy-POTH-uh-sis
Your first best guess, based on the evidence you have so far. Detectives change it when new evidence arrives.
In the sim: your Monday guess.
Shedding germs still leaving the body
Recovering people can still spread germs after they feel better.
Norovirus: contagious up to 48 hours after symptoms stop.
Patient Zero also: index case
The first recorded case in an outbreak.
Two-word quiz nickname: the doctor word + the number before one.
Part 2 · Reading the Evidence: How Case 55 Was Solved THE DETECTIVE METHOD
Suspect What the evidence said Verdict
Influenza (the flu) Fewer than 5% had a cough, and everyone recovered in 24 to 48 hours, not the 5 to 7 days flu takes. RULED OUT. Flu attacks the lungs and breathing, not the stomach.
Salmonella (food poisoning) 21 of the 55 sick students brought lunch from home, and tap water tested clean. RULED OUT. One food source hits one group, not every grade all week.
Norovirus (stomach virus) Sudden stomach sickness in 88% of cases; fast 24 to 48 hour recovery; survives days on hard plastic. CONVICTED. Its hard shell beat plain cleaning.
The Curve and How It Actually Spread

The curve (new cases): Mon 3, Tue 7, Wed 18, Thu 34, Fri 55. Bigger jumps every day prove person to person spread that is speeding up, not one fixed source like food or water.
Chain 1, the fountain: a student threw up beside the west-wing fountain. Mr. Boudreau mopped with plain water and floor soap, which only wipes germs around, and his cloth carried virus to the next tap.
Chain 2, the resin ducks: students traded small plastic ducks hand to hand all week, right before lunch. Norovirus survives for days on hard plastic, so germs rode the ducks onto fingers and straight into mouths with food.

Part 3 · Choosing Weapons: Soap vs. Sanitizer vs. Bleach KNOW WHICH TOOL AND WHY
Soap + 20 Seconds of Running Water
Soap lifts germs and grease off the skin, and running water rinses them down the drain. It removes germs instead of killing them, so it works even on tough shells.
The one tool that works on Norovirus hands. Scrub for 20 seconds.
Alcohol Hand Sanitizer
Sanitizer must kill germs with alcohol. But Norovirus has a hard protein shell with no fatty coat for the alcohol to dissolve, and dirt on hands blocks it too.
Fine for many germs. Nearly useless on stomach bugs.
Bleach Cleaner (Surfaces)
Bleach breaks the hard shell apart and kills the virus on desks, taps, and handles. Only trained adults handle bleach.
Plain soap and alcohol wipes leave live Norovirus shells on surfaces.
Part 4 · Vaccines vs. Antibiotics MEMORIZE THE FOUR ROWS
Question Vaccines (immunization) Antibiotics (medicine)
What does it do? Trains your immune system to recognize a germ and build antibodies in advance. Kills living bacteria in your body or stops them from multiplying.
When? Before you get sick. Prevents illness or makes it much milder. After you already have a bacterial infection.
Which germs? Specific viruses or bacteria (flu shot, measles shot). Bacteria ONLY. Zero effect on viruses. A cold or stomach virus will not respond.
Effect on others? Builds community immunity that shields classmates with weak immune systems. Cures one person, but overuse breeds antibiotic-resistant superbugs.

What You Must Be Able to Do on the Unit Test

Move 1, classify: read any school scenario and name the germ type (virus or bacteria), the entry gate it used (mouth, nose, eyes, or a cut), and the travel route. Remember: germs cannot soak through unbroken skin, so hands both pick germs up AND carry them in through a gate.
Move 2, choose the weapon: given a germ and a situation, pick the right prevention tool and say WHY it works (soap removes, sanitizer kills but fails on hard shells, bleach destroys shells on surfaces, vaccines train ahead of time, antibiotics cure bacteria only).
Move 3, think like the detective: read evidence (symptom percentages, recovery times, the daily curve), rule out suspects, defend the culprit, and propose a school rule that would break the chain of infection.
Also re-read Study Sheet 1: pathogen, communicable, bacteria vs. virus, the five travel routes, and the medicine rule.

Case 55's Monday Morning Game Plan (The Three Rules)

Rule 1, the 48-Hour Stay-Home Rule: stay home 48 full hours after the last symptom, because recovering students still shed contagious virus.
Rule 2, Bleach Cleaning: disinfect fountains, taps, desks, and handles with bleach and fresh cloths, because plain soap and alcohol leave tough shells alive.
Rule 3, Duck Pause + Lunch Handwashing: pause trading resin ducks and wash hands for 20 seconds with soap and running water before eating, which cuts the fomite chain and rinses virus off fingers before food.
Beyond Centennial: these habits protect classmates whose immune systems cannot fight germs for themselves, like a classmate in cancer treatment. Our habits are their shield.
