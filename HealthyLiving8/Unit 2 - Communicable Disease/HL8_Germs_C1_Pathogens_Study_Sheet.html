Part 1 · The Words to Know (Core Vocabulary) DEFINITIONS
Germs (Pathogens) say: PATH-oh-jenz
Microscopic organisms too small to see that enter the human body and cause disease.
Examples: Cold viruses, strep throat bacteria.
Communicable say: kuh-MYOO-nih-kul
Catchable / contagious. Any sickness that can spread from person to person.
Key contrast: A cold is communicable; a broken bone or asthma is not.
Bacteria say: back-TEER-ee-uh
Single-celled living organism. Complete cells that eat, grow, and reproduce on their own.
Most are harmless or helpful: yogurt cultures, gut microbiome; some cause strep throat.
Viruses say: VY-russ-ez
Not cellular · not fully alive. Microscopic genetic code in a shell.
Mechanism: Must hijack your living cells to copy itself. (Flu, COVID-19, chickenpox).
Droplets respiratory spread
Tiny wet drops of saliva and mucus expelled when coughing, sneezing, or talking loudly.
Travel: Float through shared indoor air and land in another person's nose or mouth.
Vectors biological carriers
A biting living creature that carries a germ between hosts (like a taxi for pathogens).
Real in Nova Scotia: Blacklegged deer ticks carrying Lyme disease; mosquitoes.
Part 2 · Bacteria vs. Virus: The Core Comparison MEMORIZE FOR QUIZ
Feature Bacteria Viruses
Alive? YES. Independent, one-celled living organism. NO. Not cellular — genetic instructions inside a protein coat.
Size Microscopic cell (visible under normal school microscopes). Sub-microscopic (~100x to 1000x tinier than bacteria).
How it harms you Multiplies rapidly in tissues or produces toxic wastes. Invades healthy host cells and forces them to make virus copies.
Example sicknesses Strep throat, some ear infections, Salmonella food poisoning. Colds, influenza (flu), COVID-19, chickenpox, measles.
Killed by antibiotics? YES. Antibiotics destroy cell walls and living machinery. NO. Antibiotics have zero effect. Only your immune system cures a virus.
Part 3 · The Five Travel Routes (Transmission) 5 WAYS GERMS MOVE
1. Airborne
Microscopic droplets or dust floating in shared air that get inhaled.
Colds, flu, COVID-19, measles
2. Direct Contact
Physical skin-to-skin touch between an infected person and another.
Pink eye, warts, impetigo
3. Indirect Contact
Germs waiting on shared surfaces (desks, doorknobs) or contaminated food/water.
Salmonella, E. coli, norovirus
4. Bodily Fluids
Exchange of saliva or blood (shared drink bottles, cups, toothbrushes).
Mono (kissing disease), Hepatitis B
5. Vectors
A biting insect or animal transfers pathogens directly into the bloodstream.
Ticks (Lyme disease), mosquitoes
Three Rules of Thumb That Answer Most Quiz Questions

1. Diagnostic Symptoms: Stomach troubles, food poisoning, and infected skin/throat = usually bacteria. Coughs, sneezes, fever, and spotty rashes = usually viruses.
2. Medicine Rule: Antibiotics (penicillin) cure bacterial infections, never viruses. Taking antibiotics for a cold does nothing to the cold, kills good gut bacteria, and builds resistance.
3. Entry Gateways: Pathogens need an entrance: mouth, nose, eyes, or a cut. Hands do both the travelling and the letting-in.

What You Must Be Able to Do on the Quiz

Look at any sickness or scenario and explain: (1) what type of pathogen causes it, (2) which travel route it used to reach the victim, and (3) what specific prevention action would break the chain of infection. If you can explain those three things cold, you will ace the unit quiz.
